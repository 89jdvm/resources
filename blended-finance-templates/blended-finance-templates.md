# Capital Stack Templates

## How Conservation Projects Actually Get Financed

**By Juan Diego Villacís**

I have structured financing for conservation and bioeconomy projects across the Ecuadorian Amazon. Strategic counterpart for the $1M FIEDS fund. Four investor playbooks published. Worked with the $600M Amazon Bioeconomy Fund concept. Raised founding capital for a bioeconomy export company.

Most conservation projects do not fail because of bad science or weak field teams. They fail because nobody structured the money correctly. This guide shows you how the money actually stacks.

---

## What a Capital Stack Is

A capital stack is the layered structure of all the money that funds a project. Each layer has different risk tolerance, different return expectations, and a different role.

Think of it as a building. The foundation absorbs the most risk. The floors above it take progressively less risk and expect progressively more return. If you build the upper floors first, the whole thing collapses.

In conservation finance, the capital stack answers one question: Who takes the first loss?

The answer to that question determines whether commercial money shows up. In almost every conservation project that successfully attracted private capital, a public or philanthropic funder agreed to absorb the initial losses. That agreement is what unlocked everything above it.

A single grant is not a capital stack. A single impact investor is not a capital stack. A capital stack is the intentional layering of multiple capital types, each playing a specific role, structured so that the presence of one layer makes the next layer possible.

---

## Why Conservation Projects Fail to Attract Capital

The gap between "good conservation project" and "bankable conservation project" is not about impact. It is about structure.

**The bankability gap has four dimensions:**

**1. No predictable cash flow.** Investors need to see revenue, not just outcomes. A project that protects 50,000 hectares of forest is valuable. A project that protects 50,000 hectares and generates $400K/year from certified cacao, carbon credits, and ecotourism payments is investable. The hectares are the same. The cash flow changes everything.

**2. No risk allocation.** Every project has risk. Political risk, market risk, operational risk, currency risk. The question is not whether risk exists. The question is who bears each type. When a project proposal says nothing about risk allocation, investors assume all the risk lands on them. They walk away.

**3. Wrong sequence.** A bioeconomy venture that approaches a commercial bank before securing first-loss protection from a DFI is wasting everyone's time. The bank will say no. Not because the project is bad, but because no one has de-risked it yet. Sequencing is not optional. It is the mechanism.

**4. Language mismatch.** Conservation practitioners describe ecological outcomes. Investors read financial structures. A project that "restores degraded landscapes" needs to also be a project that "generates risk-adjusted returns through diversified bioeconomy revenue streams with concessional first-loss coverage." Same project. Two languages. You need both.

## The Five Layers

Every conservation capital stack has five functional layers. Not every project needs all five at scale. But every project designer needs to understand all five and decide deliberately which ones to activate.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│          LAYER 5: RISK MITIGATION               │
│    Guarantees, insurance, political risk cover   │
│    (wraps around the entire stack)               │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│          LAYER 4: REVENUE STREAMS               │
│    Bioeconomy products, carbon credits,          │
│    PES payments, ecotourism income               │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│          LAYER 3: COMMERCIAL CAPITAL            │
│    Market-rate investors, commercial lenders,    │
│    senior debt positions                         │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│          LAYER 2: CATALYTIC CAPITAL             │
│    Impact-first investors, PRIs,                 │
│    mezzanine/subordinated debt                   │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│          LAYER 1: CONCESSIONAL CAPITAL          │
│    Grants, first-loss guarantees, DFI anchor     │
│    (absorbs the most risk, expects no return)    │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Layer 1: Concessional Capital (The Foundation)

**What it is:** Money that expects no financial return, or accepts below-market returns. Grants, first-loss tranches, technical assistance facilities.

**Who provides it:** GEF, GCF, bilateral donors (GIZ, USAID, AFD), philanthropic foundations (Moore, Packard, MacArthur), DFI concessional windows.

**What it does in the stack:** Absorbs the risk that no commercial investor will touch. If the project loses money in the first three years (and most conservation projects do), this layer takes the hit. That protection is what makes the layers above it possible.

**How much:** Typically 15-40% of total project cost in conservation blended finance structures. Higher for frontier projects in new geographies or untested models. Lower for proven models being replicated.

**Operational detail:** The FIEDS/SEMBRAR fund operates as a $1M concessional facility for territorial development in the Ecuadorian Amazon. Its role is not to fund entire projects. Its role is to absorb early-stage risk so that larger commercial and institutional capital can enter later. This is the correct use of concessional capital: as a catalyst, not as the full budget.

**What to negotiate for:** First-loss position (the concessional funder explicitly agrees to absorb losses before anyone else), technical assistance budget (separate from project capital, used to build the capacity that makes the project investable), and long tenor (5-7 years minimum for conservation projects, because ecological and economic returns do not materialize in 24-month cycles).

### Layer 2: Catalytic Capital (The Bridge)

**What it is:** Capital deployed by investors who prioritize impact but still expect some financial return. Below-market-rate, patient, flexible.

**Who provides it:** Impact-first investors (Omidyar Network, Calvert Impact), DFI investment windows (IDB Invest, IFC), program-related investments (PRIs) from foundations, social enterprise lenders (Root Capital, Incofin).

**What it does in the stack:** Sits between concessional grants and commercial capital. Takes more risk than a bank would accept. Accepts lower returns than a bank would demand. This layer is the bridge that makes the project legible to commercial investors.

**Typical instruments:** Subordinated debt (repaid after senior lenders), convertible notes, revenue-based financing, equity with capped returns.

**How much:** 20-35% of total project cost. This is often the hardest layer to fill because there are fewer catalytic capital providers than there are grant-makers or commercial lenders.

**Operational detail:** When structuring the Regeneration Opportunity playbook for Ecuadorian bioeconomy value chains, the catalytic layer was designed around revenue-based financing for producer cooperatives. A cacao cooperative does not have collateral for a traditional loan. But it does have verifiable sales contracts with export buyers. Revenue-based financing ties repayment to actual sales volume, removing the fixed-payment burden that kills agricultural enterprises during low-yield seasons.

### Layer 3: Commercial Capital (The Scale)

**What it is:** Market-rate capital that expects risk-adjusted financial returns. This is the capital that makes a project scalable.

**Who provides it:** Commercial banks (Banco Pichincha, Produbanco in Ecuador), development finance institutions at commercial terms (IDB Invest senior loans), private equity and venture capital in the bioeconomy space.

**What it does in the stack:** Provides the volume of capital needed to operate at meaningful scale. A $200K grant can fund a pilot. You need $2M-$5M in commercial capital to build processing infrastructure, certify supply chains, and access export markets.

**Why it does not show up first:** Commercial capital needs three things conservation projects rarely have at the start: predictable cash flow, clear collateral, and a track record. Layers 1 and 2 create those conditions. Without them, approaching commercial lenders is premature.

**Operational detail:** For the bamboo value chain analysis in the Regeneration Opportunity playbook, the commercial capital layer was structured around processing infrastructure. A bamboo treatment and lamination facility requires $1.5M-$3M in equipment and construction. No commercial bank will finance this without a first-loss guarantee from a DFI covering at least 20% of the loan value. With that guarantee in place, the bank's effective risk exposure drops to a level their credit committee can approve.

### Layer 4: Revenue Streams (The Engine)

**What it is:** The actual cash flow that services debt, generates returns for investors, and sustains the project beyond donor funding cycles.

This is not a capital source in the traditional sense. It is what makes the entire stack viable. Without credible revenue projections, layers 1-3 have nothing to underwrite.

**The five revenue categories for conservation projects:**

**Bioeconomy products.** Regenerative cacao, functional guayusa, wild-harvested superfoods, sustainable aquaculture (pache), bamboo construction materials. These generate recurring revenue through export sales. The Ecuadorian Amazon has at least five high-potential value chains with existing market demand in Europe and North America.

**Carbon credits.** Voluntary carbon market (VCM) and compliance market credits. Pricing varies enormously. Voluntary credits from avoided deforestation range from $5-$25/ton. Jurisdictional REDD+ credits with strong MRV can reach $15-$40/ton. The critical factor is not the price per ton. It is the MRV (monitoring, reporting, verification) system that determines whether credits are sellable.

**Payments for ecosystem services (PES).** Government or institutional payments for watershed protection, biodiversity conservation, or climate regulation. Ecuador's Socio Bosque program pays $10-$30/hectare/year for forest conservation commitments. The payments are modest. Their value in the capital stack is as predictable, government-backed baseline revenue that de-risks the rest.

**Ecotourism.** High-margin revenue in the right locations. A well-managed community ecotourism operation in the Ecuadorian Amazon generates $150-$400/visitor/day. The constraint is not demand. It is infrastructure, access, and operational consistency.

**Biodiversity credits.** An emerging asset class. Unlike carbon credits, biodiversity credits measure species outcomes and ecosystem integrity rather than tons of CO2. The market infrastructure is still developing. Pilot programs exist in Colombia, Australia, and the UK. This is a revenue stream to design for now and monetize within 3-5 years.

**Operational detail:** The strongest conservation capital stacks combine at least three revenue streams. A single revenue source creates concentration risk that sophisticated investors will flag. The MOONSHOT S.A. model combined bioeconomy product exports ($270K in EU revenue from Amazon-origin products) with supply chain premiums for deforestation-free certification. The export revenue was the primary cash flow. The certification premium was the margin enhancer. That combination is what made the unit economics work.

### Layer 5: Risk Mitigation Instruments (The Shield)

**What it is:** Financial instruments that reduce or redistribute specific risks across the stack. These wrap around the entire structure.

**The instruments that matter for conservation projects:**

**Partial credit guarantees.** A DFI guarantees 50% of a commercial loan. The bank's risk exposure is halved. The project gets financing it would not qualify for on its own. IDB Invest and MIGA (World Bank) are the primary providers for Latin American conservation projects.

**Political risk insurance.** Covers losses from government expropriation, currency transfer restrictions, or regulatory changes. MIGA provides this. Annual premiums run 0.5-1.5% of coverage amount. For projects in politically volatile regions, this is not optional.

**Currency hedging.** Conservation projects in Ecuador operate in USD (dollarized economy), which eliminates currency risk for USD-denominated investors. This is a structural advantage that most project designers undervalue. For projects in non-dollarized countries, currency hedging through TCX (The Currency Exchange Fund) adds 2-4% to financing costs but removes the risk that devaluation destroys investor returns.

**Insurance for natural events.** Parametric insurance that triggers payouts based on measurable events (drought, flood, fire) rather than assessed damage. For conservation projects, this protects against the climate risks that undermine both ecological and financial outcomes.

**Sovereign instruments.** Debt-for-nature swaps convert a portion of a country's sovereign debt into conservation funding. Ecuador executed one in 2023, converting $1.6B in debt into $450M in marine conservation funding for the Galapagos. These are nation-level instruments. Individual projects do not structure them. But understanding they exist matters because they create the macro-level funding environment that supports project-level capital stacks.

## Three Real Examples

These are composite capital stacks based on structures published in investor playbooks for Ecuadorian bioeconomy and conservation projects. The numbers reflect real project economics.

### Example 1: Regenerative Cacao Value Chain ($3.2M total)

A vertically integrated cacao operation connecting 200+ smallholder producers in the Ecuadorian Amazon to European specialty chocolate markets. Organic and deforestation-free certified.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  RISK MITIGATION                                            │
│  IDB partial credit guarantee: 50% of commercial loan       │
│  EUDR deforestation-free certification (market access)      │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  REVENUE STREAMS                                            │
│  Cacao exports to EU: $800K-$1.2M/year (at scale)          │
│  Deforestation-free premium: 15-20% above commodity price   │
│  Carbon credits from agroforestry systems: $80K-$120K/year  │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  COMMERCIAL: $1.4M (44%)                                    │
│  Commercial bank loan for processing equipment and           │
│  working capital. 5-year term, market rate.                  │
│  Secured by equipment + export contracts.                    │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  CATALYTIC: $900K (28%)                                     │
│  Revenue-based financing from impact lender.                 │
│  Repayment tied to 8% of gross cacao sales.                  │
│  No fixed monthly payment. No collateral required.           │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  CONCESSIONAL: $900K (28%)                                  │
│  GEF/bilateral grant for technical assistance,               │
│  producer training, certification costs, MRV setup.          │
│  First-loss position on the entire stack.                    │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Why this structure works:** The concessional layer covers the costs that generate no direct revenue (training, certification, monitoring). The catalytic layer finances the supply chain operations without requiring collateral that smallholders do not have. The commercial layer finances the hard assets (processing equipment) that collateralize the loan. Revenue from cacao exports services both the catalytic and commercial tranches. The partial credit guarantee is what gets the commercial bank's credit committee to approve.

**Sequencing:** Grant secured first (6-month process). Impact lender committed second, contingent on grant confirmation. Commercial bank approved last, contingent on both the guarantee and the signed export contracts.

### Example 2: Territorial Governance + Bioeconomy Platform ($5.8M total)

A multi-stakeholder territorial governance platform covering three cantons in the Ecuadorian Amazon, combining institutional coordination with diversified bioeconomy revenue streams. Modeled on the Cooperation Table architecture.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  RISK MITIGATION                                            │
│  Prefectoral decree: institutional permanence               │
│  MIGA political risk insurance: $2M coverage                │
│  Multi-stakeholder governance: 20+ signatories              │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  REVENUE STREAMS                                            │
│  Guayusa functional beverages export: $500K/year            │
│  Wild-harvested superfoods (ungurahua, morete): $200K/year  │
│  Ecotourism operations (3 lodges): $350K/year               │
│  PES/Socio Bosque payments: $180K/year                      │
│  Biodiversity credits (Year 3+): $150K/year projected       │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  COMMERCIAL: $1.7M (29%)                                    │
│  Senior debt for ecotourism infrastructure and               │
│  processing facilities. 7-year term.                         │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  CATALYTIC: $1.6M (28%)                                     │
│  Subordinated debt from impact investor.                     │
│  First repayment deferred 24 months (grace period            │
│  while revenue streams mature).                              │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  CONCESSIONAL: $2.5M (43%)                                  │
│  Bilateral cooperation (GIZ/AFD) for governance              │
│  infrastructure. Territorial trust-building.                 │
│  Technical assistance for all five value chains.             │
│  First-loss guarantee covering $800K of commercial debt.     │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Why this structure works:** The concessional share is higher (43%) because territorial governance infrastructure generates no direct revenue. It generates the conditions that make revenue possible. The Cooperation Table architecture, formalized through prefectoral decree, is the institutional backbone that coordinates land use, resolves conflicts, and provides the governance legitimacy that investors require. Five diversified revenue streams reduce concentration risk. The 24-month grace period on catalytic capital matches the realistic timeline for bioeconomy operations to reach cash flow breakeven.

**Sequencing:** Bilateral cooperation agreement signed first (12-month negotiation). Cooperation Table constituted and formalized via decree (6 months). Value chain technical assessments completed. Impact investor committed based on governance architecture and offtake agreements. Commercial debt closed last.

### Example 3: Climate-Smart Fund Vehicle ($12M total)

A structured fund pooling capital from multiple sources to invest across a portfolio of 8-12 conservation and bioeconomy projects in the Ecuadorian Amazon. Designed for institutional investors.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  RISK MITIGATION                                            │
│  Portfolio diversification across 8-12 projects             │
│  First-loss tranche absorbs first 15% of losses             │
│  Technical assistance facility (separate budget)            │
│  Currency risk: zero (dollarized economy)                   │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  REVENUE STREAMS (portfolio level)                          │
│  Aggregated bioeconomy exports: $2.5M-$4M/year             │
│  Carbon credit portfolio: $500K-$1M/year                    │
│  PES payments across territories: $300K/year                │
│  Blended IRR target: 6-9% (net of fees)                     │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  COMMERCIAL / SENIOR: $5M (42%)                             │
│  Senior notes. First claim on cash flows.                    │
│  Target return: 5-7%. Quarterly distributions.               │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  CATALYTIC / MEZZANINE: $4M (33%)                           │
│  Subordinated notes from DFI investment windows              │
│  and impact-first investors.                                 │
│  Target return: 2-4%. Absorbs losses after first-loss.       │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  CONCESSIONAL / FIRST-LOSS: $3M (25%)                       │
│  Grant-funded first-loss tranche.                            │
│  Anchor commitment from GCF or bilateral donor.              │
│  Absorbs first 15% of portfolio losses.                      │
│  $500K technical assistance facility (off-balance sheet).    │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Why this structure works:** Portfolio diversification across 8-12 projects means any single project failure does not break the fund. The 15% first-loss tranche means the senior investors can absorb significant underperformance before their principal is touched. Ecuador's dollarized economy eliminates currency risk, which is the single biggest concern for institutional investors deploying into emerging market conservation. The technical assistance facility sits off-balance sheet, funded by grants, ensuring that project-level capacity building does not dilute investor returns.

**Sequencing:** Anchor concessional commitment first (GCF or bilateral, 12-18 month process). Fund legal structure established (SPV in a jurisdiction acceptable to all investors). Catalytic investors committed based on anchor. Senior notes marketed to commercial investors with the full stack in place. Fund operational, first investments deployed within 6 months of final close.

## How to Structure Your Own

### Step 1: Start With Revenue, Not Capital

Before you approach any funder, answer this: What generates cash flow? List every revenue stream. Model each one with conservative assumptions (50-70% of optimistic case). If total projected revenue cannot service the non-concessional capital you need, your project is a grant-dependent initiative, not a blended finance deal. That is fine. But do not pretend otherwise.

### Step 2: Map Your Risk Landscape

List every risk. Political, operational, market, currency, climate, regulatory. For each risk, identify who is best positioned to bear it. Political risk goes to insurers (MIGA) or is mitigated by institutional architecture (Cooperation Tables). Market risk goes to entities with market access (exporters, offtakers). Currency risk goes to hedging facilities or disappears in dollarized economies. Operational risk stays with the project team. This mapping becomes your risk allocation table, and that table is what sophisticated investors actually evaluate.

### Step 3: Identify Your Anchor

Every capital stack needs an anchor commitment. This is the first funder who says yes. In conservation finance, the anchor is almost always concessional. A GEF grant. A bilateral cooperation agreement. A foundation's first-loss guarantee. The anchor does two things: absorbs the risk no one else will touch, and sends a credibility signal to every subsequent funder. Without an anchor, you are fundraising in a vacuum.

### Step 4: Sequence Your Asks

Go to funders in order of risk tolerance, from highest to lowest.

1. Concessional funders first. They take the most risk. They set the terms for everyone else.
2. Catalytic investors second. They commit based on the concessional layer being in place.
3. Commercial capital last. They commit based on everything below them being locked in.

Reversing this sequence is the most common mistake in conservation finance. It wastes years.

### Step 5: Build the Waterfall

The payment waterfall defines who gets paid first when revenue comes in. Standard structure:

1. Operating expenses (project must survive)
2. Senior debt service (commercial lenders, first claim)
3. Mezzanine/catalytic returns (subordinated lenders)
4. Reserve fund replenishment (buffer for lean periods)
5. Equity distributions or concessional capital recovery (if applicable)

Document this waterfall explicitly. It is what shows investors that their position in the stack is protected.

### Step 6: Stress Test

Model three scenarios:

- **Base case:** Revenue meets conservative projections. All tranches serviced.
- **Downside case:** Revenue at 60% of projections. Which tranches still get serviced? Where does the first-loss kick in?
- **Failure case:** Revenue at 30% of projections. What happens to each investor? How much does the first-loss tranche absorb? What is the recovery value of physical assets?

If your structure cannot survive the downside case without defaulting on senior obligations, you need more concessional capital or less commercial capital. Restructure before you go to market.

## The De-Risking Checklist

What makes a conservation project bankable. Score yourself honestly.

**Governance and Legal**
- [ ] Legal entity established with clean corporate structure
- [ ] Land tenure or use rights documented and uncontested
- [ ] Regulatory permits obtained or timeline confirmed
- [ ] Multi-stakeholder governance mechanism in place (Cooperation Table or equivalent)
- [ ] Community consent documented (FPIC for indigenous territories)

**Financial**
- [ ] At least two independent revenue streams identified
- [ ] Revenue projections modeled with conservative assumptions
- [ ] Unit economics validated (cost per unit vs. selling price per unit)
- [ ] Working capital needs calculated for first 18 months
- [ ] Payment waterfall defined

**Risk**
- [ ] Risk allocation table completed (each risk assigned to a bearer)
- [ ] First-loss position identified and committed
- [ ] Political risk assessed and mitigated (insurance or institutional architecture)
- [ ] Currency risk addressed (dollarization, hedging, or denomination matching)
- [ ] Climate risk covered (parametric insurance or buffer reserves)

**Operational**
- [ ] Management team with track record in place
- [ ] Supply chain mapped end-to-end
- [ ] Certifications obtained or timeline confirmed (organic, EUDR, HACCP as relevant)
- [ ] MRV system designed for carbon/biodiversity claims
- [ ] Offtake agreements signed or LOIs in hand

**Track Record**
- [ ] Pilot results documented with real numbers
- [ ] Similar project precedent cited (yours or a comparable model)
- [ ] Audited financials available (even if for a small operation)
- [ ] References from credible institutional partners

**Scoring:** Fewer than 12 boxes checked means you are not ready for commercial capital. Focus on the concessional and catalytic layers first. Between 12 and 18, you can approach impact investors but not commercial banks. Above 18, you have a bankable project.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Treating grants as the capital stack.** A $500K grant is funding. It is not a capital stack. If your entire financial plan is "get grants," you do not have a blended finance structure. You have grant dependency. That limits your scale and ties your survival to donor cycles.

**2. Approaching commercial capital too early.** The most common error. You pitch a bank before you have a first-loss guarantee. The bank says no. You conclude that "commercial capital does not work for conservation." It does. You just approached it out of sequence.

**3. Single revenue stream.** A project that depends entirely on carbon credits is one market correction away from insolvency. Diversify revenue across at least two, ideally three independent streams. The cacao revenue should still work even if carbon prices collapse.

**4. Ignoring governance as infrastructure.** Investors assess governance before they assess returns. Who makes decisions? How are conflicts resolved? What happens when a political administration changes? If you cannot answer these questions with specific institutional mechanisms, your governance is a liability, not an asset. The Cooperation Table model exists precisely to solve this.

**5. Mismatched timelines.** A 3-year grant funding a project that reaches cash flow breakeven in Year 5. A 5-year loan on infrastructure that needs 8 years to generate full returns. Timeline mismatches create liquidity crises that kill otherwise viable projects. Match your financing tenor to your economic timeline. Always.

**6. No technical assistance budget.** The biggest gap in most conservation capital stacks is not money. It is the capacity to deploy money effectively. A producer cooperative that receives a $500K catalytic investment but has no financial management systems will fail. Budget technical assistance as a separate line, funded by concessional capital, and deliver it before the commercial capital arrives.

**7. Designing the stack on paper without talking to funders.** Your capital stack is a hypothesis until you validate it with actual capital providers. Talk to the DFI program officer before you finalize the concessional layer. Talk to the impact investor before you lock the catalytic terms. Their constraints and preferences will reshape your structure. Design in conversation, not in isolation.

**8. Forgetting that AI changes the math.** Transaction costs in conservation finance are disproportionately high relative to deal size. A $3M blended finance deal requires the same legal, due diligence, and structuring work as a $30M deal. AI tools now reduce these transaction costs by 40-60% for document preparation, MRV data processing, and compliance reporting. That cost reduction is what makes smaller conservation deals viable for the first time. If your capital stack was not bankable at $3M in 2023, recalculate with AI-reduced transaction costs. The threshold has shifted.
