# EUDR From the Amazon Side

## A Compliance Field Guide Written by an Exporter, Not a Lawyer

**By Juan Diego Villacís**

I shipped nine containers of Amazon-origin products to Spain. $270K in EU export revenue. 50+ smallholder families coordinated across Ecuador and Peru. I did not read about EUDR compliance in a Brussels briefing room. I built the traceability systems, collected the GPS coordinates, and filed the documentation from the producing regions where 80% of the population has no reliable internet.

What follows is the operational sequence for building EUDR compliance from the producer side.

---

## What the EUDR Actually Requires

The EU Deforestation Regulation (Regulation 2023/1115) entered into force on June 29, 2023. The compliance deadline for large operators was December 30, 2024, later extended to December 30, 2025. For SMEs, June 30, 2025. As of April 2026, enforcement is active. If you are exporting regulated commodities to the EU, this applies to you now.

The regulation covers seven commodities: cattle, cocoa, coffee, oil palm, rubber, soy, and wood. It also covers derived products. If you export chocolate, coffee beans, palm oil, soy lecithin, timber furniture, or leather goods to the EU, this applies to you.

Three requirements must be met for any regulated product entering the EU:

**1. Deforestation-free.** The product was not produced on land that was deforested after December 31, 2020. The cutoff date is fixed. If forest was cleared on January 1, 2021 and you planted cacao there, that cacao cannot enter the EU under this regulation.

**2. Legally produced.** The product complied with all applicable laws in the country of production. This includes land use laws, environmental regulations, labor laws, tax obligations, and indigenous peoples' rights. For Ecuador, this means compliance with the Código Orgánico del Ambiente and applicable MAE/MAATE regulations.

**3. Covered by a due diligence statement.** The EU operator (your importer) must file a due diligence statement in the EU Information System before placing the product on the market. No statement, no market access.

That is it. Three requirements. The complexity is not in understanding them. It is in proving them from the field.

## Why Every Guide Gets This Wrong

Search "EUDR compliance guide" and you will find dozens of documents. Almost all of them are written by European law firms, consulting houses, or compliance software companies. They explain the regulation from the importer's perspective because the importer is the one who files the due diligence statement and faces the penalties.

Here is the problem: the importer cannot file that statement without data from you. The producer. The exporter. The person standing in the field.

The compliance burden does not stop at the EU border. It flows upstream. All the way to the smallholder family with a two-hectare cacao plot in Napo Province who has never heard of the EUDR and does not have a GPS device.

Every guide tells the importer what to demand. No guide tells the producer how to deliver it. That is what this document does.

## The Producer-Side Requirements

Your EU importer will send you a compliance questionnaire. It will ask for things that sound simple in Brussels and are brutal to collect in the field. Here is what they need from you and what each item actually means for your operation.

**Geolocation coordinates for every production plot.** Not the district. Not the canton. The actual polygon or point coordinates of each plot where the commodity was grown. For plots under four hectares, a single GPS point (latitude, longitude) is legally sufficient. For plots over four hectares, you need the polygon boundaries.

**Proof of deforestation-free status.** This means satellite imagery or land use data showing the plot was not forested after December 31, 2020. Your importer will likely cross-reference your coordinates against Global Forest Watch, Planet, or the EU Observatory datasets. If your plot shows up as forested in 2021 and cleared in 2022, that product is rejected.

**Legal compliance documentation.** Land title or legitimate use rights for each plot. Environmental permits if applicable. Proof of legal labor practices. Tax registration of the producer or cooperative.

**Traceability records.** A documented chain linking the physical product from each identified plot through aggregation, processing, and export. Lot numbers tied to specific plots and harvest dates.

**Producer identification.** Name, location, and contact information for every producer in your supply chain whose commodity ends up in the exported product.

If you work with 50 smallholder families, you need all of the above for each one. That is not an administrative task. It is a field operation.

## Geolocation: The Hard Part

Geolocation is where most Amazon-based supply chains will fail EUDR compliance. Not because the technology is difficult. Because the conditions are.

**What the regulation requires:** For plots under 4 hectares, one GPS coordinate pair (latitude, longitude) in decimal degrees. For plots over 4 hectares, a polygon defined by at least three GPS points that outline the plot boundary. Accuracy must be sufficient to identify the specific parcel of land.

**What the field looks like:** Plots accessible only by canoe. No cell signal for kilometers. Canopy cover that degrades GPS accuracy. Producers who have never used a GPS device and cannot read coordinates.

**What works:**

1. **Offline GPS apps on Android phones.** Most producers own or have access to an Android phone, even in remote areas. Apps like SW Maps, GPS Fields Area Measure, or Mapillary work offline. You download the satellite basemap over WiFi before the field visit, then collect coordinates without any cell signal. When the phone reconnects to WiFi or signal, the data syncs.

2. **Dedicated handheld GPS units.** Garmin eTrex or GPSMAP 67 units. More accurate under canopy than phone GPS. Cost is $150-$400 per unit. For a supply chain with 50+ producers, buy 3-5 units and rotate them through field teams. Do not buy one for every producer. That is not realistic.

3. **Batch collection campaigns.** Do not try to collect coordinates continuously. Organize specific field campaigns. Send a team of 2-3 people with GPS devices to a community. Spend 3-5 days collecting all plot coordinates in that area. Move to the next community. This is faster, cheaper, and more accurate than asking individual producers to self-report.

4. **Existing government or NGO data.** In Ecuador, the MAG (Ministerio de Agricultura y Ganadería) has plot data for some registered producers. SENPLADES and SigTierras have land use mapping. If your producers have catastro records, those may include georeferenced boundaries. Check before you collect from scratch.

**What does not work:**

- Asking producers to send coordinates via text message. They will send the coordinates of their house, not their plot.
- Relying on Google Maps pins dropped by someone who has never ground-truthed the location.
- Blockchain-based traceability platforms that require persistent internet for data entry. 80% of the Amazon population has no reliable internet. Blockchain solutions built in Berlin do not function in Tena.

**Accuracy tips:** Take coordinates from the center of the plot, not the edge. Under heavy canopy, wait 60 seconds for the GPS to stabilize before recording. Record at least 5 readings and average them. Mark the accuracy reading your device reports. If it shows ">15 meters," move to a clearing and try again.

## Building Traceability Without Internet

The EUDR requires a traceable link from plot to port. In the Amazon, that link must survive low connectivity, high humidity, limited literacy, and long distances between the point of harvest and the point where digital systems become available.

Here is what actually works.

**Tier 1: Paper-to-digital bridge**

At the producer level, use pre-printed carbonless copy receipt books. When a producer delivers raw material to the aggregation point, the field technician fills out a receipt: producer name, plot ID (assigned during the geolocation campaign), product type, quantity (weight), harvest date, delivery date. One copy for the producer. One copy for the aggregation point. One copy stays in the book.

At the aggregation point (the bodega, the centro de acopio, wherever product is consolidated before transport to the processing facility), a coordinator enters the receipts into a spreadsheet or database. This is where digital traceability begins.

**Critical detail:** Laminate or use waterproof receipt books. Standard paper disintegrates in 90% humidity within weeks. I learned this after losing three months of delivery records during the rainy season. Rite in the Rain brand notebooks survive. So do Tyvek-based forms.

**Tier 2: SMS and WhatsApp logging**

For cooperatives or field technicians who have intermittent signal, a structured WhatsApp message works as a traceability entry. Define a standard format:

`DELIVERY | [Producer ID] | [Plot ID] | [Product] | [Kg] | [Date]`

Example: `DELIVERY | PRD-042 | PLT-042-A | cacao seco | 85 | 2026-03-15`

The coordinator copies these messages into the central database when connectivity allows. WhatsApp messages are timestamped and attributable. They are not as clean as a database entry, but they are better than nothing, and they work with the 20% of the Amazon population that has high-quality internet.

**Tier 3: Offline-first mobile apps**

KoboToolbox and ODK Collect allow you to build custom data collection forms that work entirely offline. The field technician fills out the form on a phone or tablet. Data is stored locally. When the device connects to WiFi, it uploads to the server automatically.

For the EUDR, build a form with these fields: producer ID, plot ID, product, quantity, harvest date, delivery date, photo of the delivery (optional but useful for audits), and GPS coordinates of the delivery point.

KoboToolbox is free for humanitarian and development use. ODK Collect is open-source. Neither requires persistent connectivity.

**What ties it all together:**

A central spreadsheet or database (Google Sheets works fine for operations under 100 producers) where every delivery record is linked to a plot ID, and every plot ID is linked to GPS coordinates and the deforestation-free status verification. This is your traceability backbone. When the importer asks for it, you export the relevant rows and send them.

## The Due Diligence Statement

The due diligence statement is the importer's responsibility, not yours. But if you do not understand what it requires, you cannot provide the data your importer needs to file it.

**Who files it:** The EU operator. This is the company that first places the product on the EU market. Usually your importer or distributor.

**Where it is filed:** The EU Information System (currently under development by the European Commission). Each EU member state has a designated competent authority that manages access and enforcement.

**What the statement contains:**

1. Product description and HS code (Harmonized System tariff code)
2. Quantity and unit of measurement
3. Country of production
4. Geolocation of all plots of land where the commodity was produced
5. Time period of production (harvest dates)
6. Name and contact details of all suppliers in the chain
7. Confirmation that the product is deforestation-free (after the 2020 cutoff)
8. Confirmation that the product was produced in compliance with local laws
9. Statement that due diligence was exercised and risk is negligible, or that risk was identified and mitigated

**What this means for you:** Your importer will ask you to fill out a supplier questionnaire that maps to these fields. Respond completely and quickly. Delays in your response delay their filing. Delays in their filing delay your payment.

**Reference numbers matter.** Once filed, the due diligence statement receives a reference number. This number must accompany the product through the supply chain. If the product changes hands within the EU, the next buyer references that number. If you are shipping multiple lots from different plots, each lot may need separate documentation or a consolidated statement that covers all origins.

## Country Risk Classification

The EUDR assigns every country a risk level: low, standard, or high. This classification determines how much due diligence your importer must perform and how frequently customs will check shipments.

**What risk level means in practice:**

- **Low risk:** Simplified due diligence. Less frequent checks. Faster market entry. 1% of imports checked.
- **Standard risk:** Full due diligence as described in this guide. 3% of imports checked.
- **High risk:** Enhanced due diligence. More frequent and thorough checks. Up to 9% of imports checked. Additional verification requirements.

**Ecuador's status:** As of April 2026, the European Commission has not published final country benchmarks. Build your compliance systems for the "standard" tier. If Ecuador receives "low" classification, you will have over-prepared. If it receives "high," you will be glad you did not wait. Do not build your compliance plan around a classification that does not yet exist.

**What this means for your operation:** If Ecuador is classified as standard, your compliance burden is the baseline described in this guide. If it is classified as high risk, expect your importer to require additional documentation: independent third-party verification of deforestation-free status, more granular satellite imagery analysis, and possibly on-site audits.

**The practical implication:** Do not wait for the classification to be finalized before building your compliance systems. Build for "standard" now. If the classification comes back as "low," you are over-prepared and your data becomes a competitive advantage. If it comes back as "high," you are already 80% of the way there instead of starting from zero.

## A Practical Compliance Checklist

This checklist assumes you are a producer or exporter supplying an EU importer. Work through it sequentially.

### Phase 1: Mapping (Months 1-2)

- [ ] Compile a complete list of every producer in your supply chain with name, ID number, and contact information
- [ ] Assign a unique Producer ID and Plot ID to each producer and each plot
- [ ] Plan field campaigns for GPS coordinate collection. Budget 3-5 days per community cluster
- [ ] Acquire GPS equipment: 3-5 handheld units or configure Android phones with offline GPS apps (SW Maps or GPS Fields Area Measure)
- [ ] Download satellite basemaps for your operating area while connected to WiFi
- [ ] Collect GPS coordinates for every plot. Single point for plots under 4 hectares. Polygon for plots over 4 hectares
- [ ] Record coordinates in decimal degrees (e.g., -1.2345, -77.8765). Not degrees-minutes-seconds
- [ ] Verify coordinates against satellite imagery (Google Earth Pro is free) to confirm the point falls on agricultural land, not forest

### Phase 2: Deforestation Verification (Month 2-3)

- [ ] Cross-reference all plot coordinates against Global Forest Watch (globalforestwatch.org). Check for tree cover loss after December 31, 2020
- [ ] For any plots flagging forest loss, investigate: Was it actual deforestation or a data error? GFW occasionally flags agroforestry or secondary growth clearing as deforestation
- [ ] Gather supporting evidence for flagged plots: historical satellite imagery from Google Earth Pro (free, has a time slider), Agrocalidad records, municipal land use records
- [ ] Remove any plots from your supply chain that cannot demonstrate deforestation-free status. Do not try to hide them. If one contaminated lot enters your batch, the entire shipment is non-compliant

### Phase 3: Legal Documentation (Months 2-3)

- [ ] Collect proof of land tenure or legitimate use rights for each producer: title, lease agreement, communal land assignment, or usufruct documentation
- [ ] Verify each producer's tax registration (RUC or RISE in Ecuador)
- [ ] Confirm environmental permits are current if applicable (MAE/MAATE)
- [ ] Document labor compliance: no child labor, compliance with minimum wage requirements, employment contracts where applicable
- [ ] Compile municipal or cantonal permits for agricultural activity

### Phase 4: Traceability System (Months 3-4)

- [ ] Design and print waterproof delivery receipt books (carbonless copy, three-part)
- [ ] Train all field technicians on filling out receipts: producer ID, plot ID, product, quantity, date
- [ ] Establish the aggregation point data entry process: receipts to spreadsheet within 48 hours of delivery
- [ ] Set up the central database or Google Sheet with fields: Producer ID, Plot ID, GPS coordinates, product, quantity, harvest date, delivery date, lot number
- [ ] Define lot numbering system that links processing batches to specific deliveries and plots
- [ ] Test the system with a real delivery cycle before the importer asks for it

### Phase 5: Importer Coordination (Month 4-5)

- [ ] Request your importer's EUDR compliance questionnaire or supplier template
- [ ] Fill it out with the data you have collected. Do not leave fields blank. If something is not applicable, explain why
- [ ] Provide GPS coordinate files in the format the importer requests (CSV with latitude/longitude columns is standard)
- [ ] Share your traceability database or a summary export covering the lots you plan to ship
- [ ] Agree on the documentation package that will accompany each shipment
- [ ] Confirm the importer understands the due diligence statement must be filed before the product clears customs

### Phase 6: Ongoing Compliance (Continuous)

- [ ] Update GPS data when producers add or change plots
- [ ] Re-verify deforestation-free status annually using updated satellite data
- [ ] Maintain delivery records for every production cycle
- [ ] Archive all documentation for five years (the EUDR requires operators to keep records for five years)
- [ ] Conduct internal audits: Can you trace a random kilo of product from the export container back to a specific plot? If not, fix the gap

## What Will Go Wrong

I have been through this process. Here is what will break.

**GPS collection will take three times longer than planned.** Producers will not be available when you arrive. Plots are further apart than the map shows. Rain stops fieldwork for days. Budget for delays. If you plan two weeks, budget four.

**Some producers will refuse to share plot coordinates.** They will worry about land disputes, government interference, or losing access to their plots. This is a trust problem, not a technology problem. Explain why you need the coordinates, who will see them, and what happens if you cannot collect them (they cannot sell to you for EU markets). Field technicians who have existing relationships with the community get better results than outside consultants.

**Your traceability records will have gaps.** Especially in the first cycle. A field technician forgets a receipt. A WhatsApp message uses the wrong format. A delivery is recorded without a plot ID. Build in a monthly review process where someone checks the database for missing fields and follows up. Gaps are normal. Unfixed gaps are the problem.

**Satellite imagery will flag plots that are not deforested.** Agroforestry systems (shade-grown cacao under forest canopy) sometimes register as "forest" in one year and "not forest" in another as canopy density changes. This creates false positives on Global Forest Watch. You will need to provide ground-truth evidence: photos, agronomist reports, or historical land use documentation. Plan for 10-15% of your plots to require this kind of follow-up.

**Your importer will change requirements mid-process.** The EU Information System is still being finalized. Implementation guidance from member states will evolve. Your importer's compliance team will update their questionnaire. Build your data collection to be flexible. Collect more data than the minimum. It is much easier to filter a comprehensive dataset down than to go back to the field for one missing data point.

**Humidity will destroy records.** I said this already but it bears repeating. If your aggregation point is in the Amazon, paper records left unprotected will be unreadable within one rainy season. Use waterproof materials, store in sealed containers, and digitize within 48 hours.

**The cost will fall on you, not the importer.** The EUDR places the legal obligation on the EU operator. But the cost of collecting geolocation data, building traceability systems, training producers, and maintaining records falls on the supply chain. On you. On the cooperative. On the producers earning $2-5 per kilo. Factor EUDR compliance costs into your pricing from the start. Do not absorb them as a cost of doing business and hope the importer pays more later. Negotiate compliance cost-sharing upfront, in writing.
