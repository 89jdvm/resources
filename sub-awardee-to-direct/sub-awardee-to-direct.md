# The Direct Recipient Roadmap

## Graduate From Sub-Awardee to Direct DFI Funding

**By Juan Diego Villacís**

Here is how the system works right now.

An international donor pledges $5 million for indigenous territorial conservation in the Amazon. The money goes to an INGO based in Washington, London, or Geneva. That INGO takes a 15-25% management fee. It hires staff. It sets up an office. It creates the reporting templates. Then it sub-awards the remaining funds to the indigenous organization that was supposed to receive the money in the first place.

The indigenous organization ends up as a contractor on its own territorial project. It follows someone else's reporting templates. It operates on someone else's disbursement timeline. It builds someone else's institutional capacity metrics.

This is not malice. It is a structural consequence of the fiduciary requirements that DFIs impose. The requirements are real. The question is whether indigenous organizations can meet them directly. They can. But it takes specific, sequenced work.

This roadmap lays out that sequence.

---

## Why Organizations Get Stuck at Sub-Awardee

USAID's own learning documents describe the pattern explicitly. When indigenous umbrella organizations like COICA attempt to partner directly with bilateral agencies, the programmatic co-creation succeeds. The partnership design works. Then the multi-million dollar award gets blocked because the local entity's financial and administrative systems are deemed incapable of absorbing direct funding.

The organization accepts sub-awardee status beneath a Global North INGO. The INGO becomes prime contractor to handle financial compliance.

The five capabilities that DFIs assess before granting direct access:

1. **Accounting systems** with auditable trails
2. **Procurement policies** with documented competitive processes
3. **Governance structures** with segregation of duties and documented decisions
4. **Grant management track record** with clean financial and narrative close-outs
5. **Compliance framework** including anti-fraud, conflict of interest, and whistleblower policies

These are not arbitrary. Each one corresponds to a specific fiduciary risk that the DFI is legally required to mitigate. Understanding this removes the mystery. It is not a cultural test. It is a risk management checklist.

## The Five Stages

### Stage 1: Foundation (Months 1-3)

Build the institutional documentation that does not require money to create. It requires discipline.

**Deliverables:**
- Written financial policies and procedures manual. Template it on your fiscal sponsor's version if you have one. Include: approval thresholds, segregation of duties (who authorizes, who pays, who records), petty cash management, bank reconciliation frequency.
- Conflict of interest policy. Every board member and senior staff signs it.
- Organizational chart with job descriptions and signing authorities.
- Procurement policy. Define thresholds. Under $500: single source. $500-$5,000: three quotes. Over $5,000: competitive bidding with evaluation criteria.

**What actually happens:** This stage is boring and essential. Most organizations skip it because the documentation feels bureaucratic. It is. It is also the first thing every DFI assessor asks for. Without it, the assessment stops on day one.

### Stage 2: Systems (Months 3-6)

Move from paper records to auditable digital systems.

**Deliverables:**
- Implement accounting software. QuickBooks, Wave (free), or Xero. Not Excel. The software creates the audit trail automatically.
- Open an institutional bank account with dual-signatory controls for withdrawals above your defined threshold.
- Set up a digital filing system for receipts, contracts, and correspondence. Each transaction: receipt, approval, journal entry, filed in one place.
- Register for tax compliance in your jurisdiction. File back taxes if needed.

**What actually happens:** The jump from paper to software is where most capacity-building programs stall. The problem is usually not the software. It is that the person responsible for financial management is also the program coordinator, the communications officer, and the driver. You need a dedicated finance person. Even part-time. Even shared with another organization.

### Stage 3: Track Record (Months 6-18)

Build the grant management history that DFIs evaluate.

**Deliverables:**
- Apply for and manage 2-3 small grants ($10K-$50K) with formal reporting requirements. Entry points for Latin American organizations: US Embassy Democracy and Human Rights Fund (typically $10-25K), GIZ bilateral small grants, Swiss Development Cooperation small grants program, Ford Foundation BUILD program for organizational strengthening. In Ecuador specifically: Fondo Ambiental Nacional, FONAG micro-grants, and provincial government cooperation funds.
- Close each grant with a clean final narrative and financial report. Get the acceptance letter from the funder. File it.
- Request an independent audit after your first year of operations with external funding. Even a limited-scope audit demonstrates commitment to accountability.

**What actually happens:** This is the stage where you prove it. Not to the DFI you eventually want to access. To yourself. The first clean close teaches you where your systems break. The second one confirms the fix worked. By the third, you have a process.

The most common failure at this stage: over-committing. Organizations accept three grants simultaneously with different reporting templates and burn out their finance staff. Take them sequentially until your systems can handle parallel grants.

### Stage 4: Assessment (Months 18-24)

Prepare for and pass a formal organizational capacity assessment.

**Deliverables:**
- Request a pre-award assessment from the DFI you want to access. USAID uses the Organizational Capacity Assessment (OCA). Other DFIs have their own instruments.
- Before the assessment, conduct an internal self-evaluation using the Direct Funding Readiness Score tool.
- Address every gap identified. Common gaps at this stage: missing board meeting minutes from early months (go back and document retroactively if needed), incomplete procurement files for early purchases, outdated policies that were written in Stage 1 but never updated.

**What actually happens:** The assessment is not a pass/fail exam. It produces findings. Some findings are "conditions" (must fix before receiving funds). Others are "recommendations" (should fix, but not blocking). The conditions are your work plan. Focus on closing conditions. Recommendations can wait.

If you do not pass on the first attempt, that is normal. The assessment tells you exactly what to fix. Fix it. Request a follow-up assessment.

### Stage 5: Direct Access (Months 24+)

Apply for direct funding.

**Deliverables:**
- Respond to calls for proposals as prime applicant, not as sub-awardee.
- Use the DFI Decoder guide to frame your proposal correctly.
- Cite your track record: number of grants managed, total value, clean audit opinions, OCA results.

**What actually happens:** The first direct award is the hardest. After that, every subsequent funder sees your track record and the barrier drops. The compound effect is real: each clean close makes the next application easier.

## What Each Major DFI Requires

| DFI | Assessment Instrument | Key Requirements | Typical Threshold |
|-----|----------------------|------------------|-------------------|
| **USAID** | Organizational Capacity Assessment (OCA) | Accounting systems, procurement policies, governance, HR, M&E | Varies by mission. $500K+ awards trigger full assessment. |
| **GEF** | Fiduciary Standards Assessment | Financial management, project oversight, anti-corruption | Applies to all executing agencies. |
| **GCF (Green Climate Fund)** | Accreditation process | Fiduciary, ESG safeguards, gender policy | Micro: up to $10M. Small: $10-50M. |
| **EU** | PRAG compliance + Pillar Assessment | Accounting, internal controls, audit, procurement | 6-pillar assessment for large grants. |
| **IDB** | Country-specific fiduciary requirements | Financial management systems, audit, procurement | Varies by project size and instrument. |

## The Three Shortcuts That Do Not Work

**1. Hiring a compliance consultant to write your policies.**
They will produce beautiful documents. Your staff will not follow them because they did not write them. The DFI assessor will ask your finance officer to explain the procurement process. If the finance officer refers to the manual instead of describing what they actually do, the assessor knows the policy is decorative.

Build the policies with your team. It takes longer. It sticks.

**2. Partnering with an INGO "temporarily" while you build capacity.**
Temporary becomes permanent. The INGO has no incentive to make itself unnecessary. After three years, you are still a sub-awardee with the same capacity gaps because the INGO's systems have been doing the work.

Use fiscal sponsors for early grants. But build your own systems in parallel from day one.

**3. Waiting until you feel "ready" to apply.**
You will never feel ready. The first assessment will find gaps. That is its purpose. Apply when you have completed Stage 3. The assessment is the diagnostic, not the destination.
