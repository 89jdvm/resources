Tool 08

Seed to Shelf

Every step from smallholder family to European retail. Certifications, timelines, costs, logistics. Built from doing it, not from reading the regulations.

By Juan Diego Villacis. Founder of MOONSHOT S.A. $270K in EU export revenue. 9 containers to Spain. 50+ smallholder families coordinated.

Format: 8-stage export roadmap with costs Audience: Bioeconomy exporters, cooperatives License: CC BY 4.0

Who this is for

You have a product from the Amazon or a similar origin territory. You have producers. You may even have buyers interested. But you have never actually shipped a container to Europe and you do not know the full sequence of what that requires: the certifications, the regulatory compliance, the logistics, the capital. This roadmap covers every stage from validating your product to scaling repeat shipments.

From experience

This is not compiled from regulation databases. It is built from shipping 9 containers of Amazonian products from Ecuador to Spain, coordinating 50+ smallholder families, and making every mistake on this list at least once.

The eight stages

Do not start with production. Start with the buyer. Find buyers first, send samples, get pricing feedback, and work backwards from what the market will pay to what your supply chain needs to deliver.

Send 3-5 samples to potential distributors in your target market. Ask for pricing feedback, not commitment. You need to know the landed price they expect before you can calculate whether your margins work.

Work backwards from the buyer's required spec: packaging format, minimum order quantity, delivery frequency, shelf life requirements. These constraints define your entire production and logistics chain.

What I learned

First container was cacao snacks, thin margin. By the 4th container, I shifted to higher-value functional ingredients. The product that sells easiest is rarely the product with the best margin. Validate both desirability and unit economics before committing to production infrastructure.

In Ecuador, you need three things before you can legally export: SRI registration (tax authority) with export activity codes, Ecuapass registration (customs platform) with your digital signature, and Agrocalidad registration if your product is food, agricultural, or plant-based.

Timeline: 2-4 weeks if your corporate documents are clean. 2-3 months if you have issues with your RUC, your legal representative's signature, or your Agrocalidad facility inspection.

Do not underestimate the Agrocalidad facility inspection. They will visit your processing facility and it must meet BPM (Good Manufacturing Practices) standards before they issue your export registration. If your facility is not ready, this single step can delay your entire timeline by months.

Common bottleneck

Digital signatures for Ecuapass expire and must be renewed annually. If your legal representative changes, you need a new signature. Budget time for this administrative step; it has delayed more first containers than any certification.

The gap between "we know how to produce" and "same spec every batch" is enormous. This is where most smallholder-based export operations fail, not at the border, but in the production facility.

You need written Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) for every step of your production process. Not because auditors require them (they do), but because without them you cannot guarantee the consistency that repeat buyers demand.

BPM (Buenas Practicas de Manufactura) is the baseline. It covers facility hygiene, personnel practices, equipment maintenance, pest control, water quality, waste management, and documentation. In Ecuador, BPM certification is a prerequisite for Agrocalidad export registration and for HACCP certification.

  • Document every process. Receiving raw material, storage conditions, processing steps, packaging, labeling, dispatch.
  • Train every operator. Written SOPs mean nothing if the people doing the work have not been trained on them.
  • Record every batch. Traceability starts here: lot numbers, dates, quantities, supplier IDs.

Certifications are the gatekeepers of European retail. Which ones you need depends on your buyer, your product category, and your target channel.

The recertification trap

Certifications are annual. Budget the cost of recertification before you receive your first certificate. Many exporters celebrate getting HACCP certified without realizing they need to pay again in 12 months. Build recertification into your annual operating costs from day one.

Sequence matters. BPM first (it is a prerequisite for HACCP). HACCP second (most EU buyers require it at minimum). BRCGS only if your target buyer requires it. Organic only if your supply chain can genuinely support it and the price premium justifies the cost.

EU labelling requirements are specific and enforced at the border. Your label must include:

  • Product name (legal name under EU food law)
  • List of ingredients (descending order by weight)
  • Allergen declaration (bold text)
  • Net quantity
  • Date of minimum durability or use-by date
  • Storage conditions
  • Country of origin
  • Nutritional declaration (per 100g and optionally per serving)
  • Name and address of the EU-based food business operator
  • Lot number for traceability
  • Language requirements (language of the destination market)

The last point is critical: your label must be in the language of the country where the product is sold. If you are selling in Spain, the label is in Spanish. Germany, in German. If your distributor covers multiple markets, you may need multilingual labels.

Learned the hard way

I reprinted 4,000 labels for the 3rd container because I switched distributors and the new distributor required different label information (their company name and address as the EU food business operator). Budget label redesign into every distributor change.

Container options: 20ft standard (most common for first shipments), 40ft standard, 40ft high cube, or refrigerated (reefer) if your product requires temperature control. A 20ft container holds roughly 10-12 pallets.

Route: Ecuador to Spain, 18-25 days depending on the shipping line, port of origin (Guayaquil), and whether there are transshipment stops (Panama is common).

Documents per shipment:

  • Commercial invoice
  • Packing list
  • Bill of lading (B/L)
  • Certificate of origin
  • Phytosanitary certificate (Agrocalidad)
  • HACCP or food safety certificate
  • Lab analysis results (microbiological, heavy metals as applicable)
  • EUR.1 movement certificate (for tariff preferences under trade agreements)

Customs broker relationship: Find a customs broker in Ecuador who has processed food exports to the EU before. Not all customs brokers are equal. A broker who specializes in mining exports will not know the phytosanitary requirements for food products. Interview at least three. Ask for references from other food exporters.

Port timing

Book your container slot 2-3 weeks before your production is finished. Port congestion in Guayaquil can add 3-7 days. If you are shipping perishable or time-sensitive products, this buffer is not optional.

The EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) applies to cacao, coffee, soy, palm oil, wood, rubber, and cattle products entering the EU market. If your export product falls under these categories, you need to comply.

What it requires:

  • Geolocation data for every plot of land where your raw material was produced. GPS coordinates, polygon boundaries for plots larger than 4 hectares.
  • Deforestation-free verification proving the land was not deforested after December 31, 2020.
  • Due diligence statement filed in the EU's information system before the product enters the market.

The compliance burden falls on the exporter and the importer jointly, but in practice, the exporter does most of the work because the data originates at the producer level.

Zero-Knowledge Verification

One concept worth exploring: systems that allow producers to prove compliance (deforestation-free status, legal land use) without revealing the exact location of their plots to every actor in the supply chain. This protects producer privacy while satisfying regulatory requirements. The technology exists; the implementation frameworks are still emerging.

For the full compliance checklist and field-level implementation guide, see EUDR From the Amazon Side.

Your first container is a proof of concept. The question is whether your operation can sustain repeat shipments. Several dynamics shift once you move from one-off to ongoing:

  • Payment terms shift. First container, buyers may pay upfront or on short terms. By the third container, they expect 30-60-90 day payment terms. You need working capital to cover production and shipping while waiting for payment.
  • Pre-financing needs. Smallholder families need to be paid at harvest, not when your buyer pays you 90 days later. The gap between when you pay producers and when you receive payment is the single biggest cash flow challenge in this model.
  • Quality consistency. One bad container can end a buyer relationship. As you scale, quality control becomes exponentially harder because you are aggregating from more producers with more variation.
  • Working capital gap: 90+ days is typical. You pay producers at harvest, pay for processing, pay for shipping, and then wait 60-90 days for the buyer to pay. That gap can be $30K-$80K per container depending on your product.

The trust machine

At scale, this operation runs on trust at every link. Producers trust you will pay fair prices. You trust producers will deliver consistent quality. Buyers trust you will deliver on time and on spec. Financiers trust you will repay. Every broken link collapses the chain. Building that trust takes years, not contracts.
