# Seed to Shelf

## The Complete Export Roadmap From Amazon Producers to European Markets

**By Juan Diego Villacís**

I founded MOONSHOT S.A. and built Kitu Snacks from zero. $270K in EU export revenue. Nine containers shipped to Spain. 50+ smallholder families and cooperatives coordinated across Ecuador and Peru. HACCP and BPM certified. EUDR deforestation-free compliant.

Every step of this supply chain broke at least once. Every certification took longer than expected. Every container taught me something the regulations did not mention.

This roadmap is what I wish someone had given me before the first shipment.

---

## The 8 Stages

### Stage 1: Product Validation

Before you spend a dollar on certification, answer one question: Does a buyer in Europe want this product at a price that covers your costs?

This is where most bioeconomy ventures fail. They develop a product, certify it, ship it, and then discover there is no buyer at their price point.

**What to do:**
- Identify 3-5 potential importers or distributors in your target EU market. Spain, Germany, the Netherlands, and France are the most receptive to Amazon-origin products.
- Send samples. Not a business plan. Samples. Importers evaluate with their hands, not with your deck.
- Get pricing feedback. The importer will tell you what they can pay per kilo delivered to their warehouse (CIF or DDP). Work backwards from that number to determine if your production cost, certification cost, logistics cost, and margin make the product viable.
- If the math does not work at current volume, you need either more volume (aggregate more producers) or a higher-value product category (functional foods, superfoods, specialty ingredients).

**What I learned:** Our first container was cacao-based snacks. The margin was thin. By the fourth container, we had shifted the product mix toward higher-value functional ingredients where the margin justified the logistics cost. Validate the economics before you certify.

### Stage 2: Legal Structure and Export Registration

You cannot export from Ecuador without a legal entity registered with the following:

- **SRI (tax authority):** Active RUC with export activity codes.
- **Ecuapass:** Ecuador's customs portal. All export declarations, phytosanitary certificates, and certificates of origin are processed here.
- **Agrocalidad:** If you are exporting food or agricultural products, you need Agrocalidad registration as an exporter. This requires a facility inspection.

**Timeline:** 2-4 weeks if your documentation is clean. 2-3 months if you need to resolve SRI inconsistencies.

**What I learned:** Register your export activity code correctly from the start. Changing it later triggers a review process that can delay your first shipment by weeks.

### Stage 3: Production Standardization

European buyers require consistency. Not artisanal variation. Consistency.

**What to do:**
- Establish standard operating procedures (SOPs) for every production step: harvest, post-harvest handling, drying, processing, packaging.
- Implement BPM (Buenas Prácticas de Manufactura / Good Manufacturing Practices). This is the baseline for any food export. It covers: facility hygiene, personnel hygiene, pest control, water quality, waste management, and traceability at the production level.
- Train every producer in the supply chain on the SOPs. Not once. Repeatedly. Consistency comes from repetition, not from documents.

**What I learned:** The gap between "we know how to produce this" and "we can produce this to the same specification every batch" is enormous. Our first container had quality variation that the Spanish distributor flagged. It took three production cycles to standardize.

### Stage 4: Certification

The certifications you need depend on your product and your target market. Here are the ones I navigated:

**HACCP (Hazard Analysis Critical Control Points):**
- Required by most EU importers for processed food products.
- It is a risk-based system. You identify every point in your production process where a hazard could occur, and you define controls for each one.
- Cost: $3,000-$8,000 for initial certification depending on complexity.
- Timeline: 3-6 months including implementation and audit.

**BPM (Good Manufacturing Practices):**
- Prerequisite for HACCP. Must be in place before the HACCP audit.
- Ecuador's ARCSA (health authority) issues the BPM certification for food processing facilities.

**BRCGS (British Retail Consortium Global Standards):**
- Higher tier. Required by major European retail chains (Tesco, Carrefour, Aldi).
- If you are selling to distributors who supply these chains, they may require BRCGS.
- Cost: $10,000-$20,000 for implementation and certification.
- I led the BRCGS implementation process at MOONSHOT. It is significantly more demanding than HACCP.

**Organic Certification (optional but high-value):**
- EU organic regulation requires annual inspection by an accredited certifier.
- For smallholder families: group certification is possible. One certificate covers the entire producer group under a documented Internal Control System (ICS).
- Cost: $5,000-$15,000 annually for group certification depending on number of producers.

**What I learned:** Certifications are not one-time. They are annual. Budget for recertification before you budget for the first certificate. The cost of maintaining certifications is a permanent line item, not a startup expense.

### Stage 5: Labelling and EU Regulatory Compliance

EU labelling regulations are extremely specific. One error on the label can cause a container to be held at customs.

**Mandatory label elements for food products entering the EU:**
- Product name (legal denomination per EU regulation)
- Ingredient list in descending order of weight
- Allergen declaration (bold or highlighted)
- Net quantity
- Date of minimum durability ("best before") or "use by" date
- Storage conditions
- Name and address of the EU-based food business operator (your importer)
- Country of origin
- Nutritional declaration (per 100g/ml, in the prescribed format)
- Lot number for traceability

**What I learned:** The importer's name and address must appear on the label as the responsible EU food business operator. If your importer changes, your labels change. We had to reprint 4,000 labels for our third container because we switched distributors. Build label flexibility into your production process.

### Stage 6: Logistics and Shipping

**Container options:**
- 20-foot container: 18-22 pallets, ~20 tons. Most common for food exports from Ecuador.
- 40-foot container: for larger volumes. Rarely necessary in early-stage bioeconomy exports.
- Consolidation (groupage): share a container with other exporters. Lower cost, longer transit time, less control.

**Route from Ecuador to Spain:**
- Port: Guayaquil (main export port).
- Transit time to Barcelona or Valencia: 18-25 days depending on shipping line and route.
- Incoterm: FOB Guayaquil (you deliver to the port, buyer assumes cost and risk from there) or CIF destination (you cover freight and insurance).

**Documents required per shipment:**
- Commercial invoice
- Packing list
- Bill of lading
- Certificate of origin (issued via Ecuapass)
- Phytosanitary certificate (Agrocalidad)
- HACCP certificate
- Laboratory analysis report (depending on product category)

**What I learned:** Build your relationship with your customs broker in Guayaquil before your first shipment. A good broker prevents delays. A bad one creates them. We used the same broker for all nine containers. They knew our documentation flow and caught errors before customs did.

### Stage 7: EUDR Compliance (2024 onward)

The EU Deforestation Regulation requires all importers to prove that products entering the EU did not contribute to deforestation after December 31, 2020.

For Amazon-origin products, this means:
- Geolocation data for every production plot (GPS coordinates)
- Due diligence statement filed by the EU importer
- Risk assessment based on country of origin

**See the companion resource "EUDR From the Amazon Side" for the full field guide.**

**What I learned:** The EUDR is written for European importers. The compliance burden flows upstream to producers. If your smallholder families cannot provide geolocation data for their plots, you cannot export under the EUDR. Build the data collection into your producer onboarding process from day one.

### Stage 8: Scaling and Sustainability

Your first container is a proof of concept. Your third container is a business.

**What changes between container 1 and container 9:**
- Payment terms shift from advance payment to 30-60 day net terms. Your cash flow management becomes the bottleneck.
- Producers need reliable pre-financing to produce at the volume you need. Build a revolving fund or negotiate pre-harvest advances with your buyer.
- Quality consistency becomes your reputation. One bad container can end a distributor relationship.
- You need working capital. The gap between paying producers and receiving payment from your buyer can be 90+ days.

**What I learned:** The supply chain is a trust machine. The producers trust you to pay on time. The buyer trusts you to deliver consistent quality. You trust the logistics chain to not lose your container. Every broken promise costs more than the monetary value of that transaction because it erodes the trust that the entire system runs on.

## The Numbers Nobody Shares

| Item | Approximate Cost (Ecuador to Spain) |
|------|--------------------------------------|
| BPM implementation and certification | $2,000 - $5,000 |
| HACCP implementation and certification | $3,000 - $8,000 |
| BRCGS implementation and certification | $10,000 - $20,000 |
| Organic group certification (annual) | $5,000 - $15,000 |
| Label design and printing (per SKU) | $500 - $2,000 |
| Laboratory analysis (per batch) | $200 - $800 |
| Customs broker (per shipment) | $300 - $600 |
| Freight (20ft container, Guayaquil to Barcelona) | $2,500 - $4,500 |
| Insurance (per container) | $200 - $500 |
| Total first-container cost (including certification) | $15,000 - $35,000 |

These numbers are from 2023-2025 and will vary. But the order of magnitude is correct. If someone tells you that you can export from the Amazon to Europe for $5,000, they have not done it.
