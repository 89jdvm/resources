# Power Mapping for Conservation Platforms

## See Who Actually Holds Influence Before Your First Meeting

**By Juan Diego Villacís** -- Practitioner-Scholar for AI + Amazonian Governance

I coordinated 22+ organizations per Cooperation Table across 5 sectors in 3 provinces of the Ecuadorian Amazon. Public sector, indigenous authorities, NGOs, academia, private sector. Every time, the first thing I had to figure out was the same: who actually holds power here, and what kind of power do they hold?

This resource is the tool I wish I had before my first stakeholder mapping session. It would have saved me two months of political miscalculations in Morona Santiago.

---

## Why Power Mapping Before Convening

Most practitioners build their stakeholder list by asking "who works in this territory?" That produces a roster of 40-60 organizations, no sense of hierarchy, and a first meeting where the wrong person feels excluded and quietly kills your platform from the outside. Power mapping forces you to answer a different question: who can make this platform succeed, and who can make it fail? You map that before you send a single invitation. The government official who controls the budget is not necessarily the person who controls whether people show up. The NGO with the biggest grant portfolio may have less territorial influence than a community federation with no formal legal status. If you do not understand these dynamics before convening, you will design a platform that looks legitimate on paper but has no traction on the ground.

---

## The Four Dimensions of Power in Conservation Platforms

In most conservation territories, power does not live in one place. It is distributed across four dimensions, and they almost never overlap in the same organization. Understanding which organization holds which type of power is the entire game.

### 1. Administrative Authority

Who can issue permits, allocate budgets, and enforce regulations.

In the Ecuadorian Amazon, this is the provincial government (GAD Provincial), line ministries (Environment, Agriculture, Planning), and sometimes municipal governments. The Prefect's office in Morona Santiago controlled the annual budget for conservation-related activities and had the authority to sign inter-institutional agreements. Without their signature, no constitutive agreement had legal standing. But administrative authority alone does not mean people will show up to your table. In Sucumbios, the provincial government had full administrative authority and almost no convening power among indigenous communities.

### 2. Territorial Legitimacy

Who has historical and legal claim to the land.

In the Amazon, indigenous nations hold territorial sovereignty and FPIC (Free, Prior, and Informed Consent) rights. This is not symbolic. In Morona Santiago, the Shuar and Achuar nations hold significant territorial influence over vast areas that overlap with the provincial government's administrative boundary. Any conservation platform that does not include them as co-equal governance partners is building on someone else's land without permission. Territorial legitimacy also extends to colonist communities that have held land titles for generations, even without indigenous sovereignty claims.

### 3. Technical Capacity

Who has data, expertise, and implementation capability.

This is usually where INGOs and academia sit. Conservation International had the monitoring systems, the GIS data, and the project management infrastructure. Universities like IKIAM and UEA had research capacity and baseline data. Local NGOs like Fundacion Pachamama had community-level relationships that larger organizations lacked. Technical capacity matters because whoever holds the data often shapes the narrative. The organization that produces the deforestation report or the biodiversity assessment frames the problem, and framing the problem is a form of power.

### 4. Financial Leverage

Who controls or channels the money.

Follow the funding and you find the real influence map. In the Cooperation Tables, Conservation International channeled the primary grant funding. But the provincial government controlled public investment budgets that dwarfed NGO grants. Indigenous federations like CONFENIAE were increasingly positioned as direct recipients of climate finance. Private sector cooperatives controlled economic activity (cacao, naranjilla, coffee) that determined livelihoods. Financial leverage is not just about who has the biggest budget. It is about who can redirect money toward or away from your platform's priorities.

## The Power Mapping Canvas

Plot each stakeholder on two axes. This creates four quadrants, and each quadrant tells you exactly how to engage that organization.

**Axis 1: Formal Authority** (vertical, low to high)
Does this organization have legal, regulatory, or institutional authority over the territory or issue? Can they issue permits, allocate budgets, sign binding agreements, or block activities?

**Axis 2: Practical Influence** (horizontal, low to high)
Does this organization shape what actually happens on the ground? Do people show up when they convene? Do communities trust them? Can they mobilize resources, data, or people?

### Quadrant I: High Authority, High Influence -- ANCHOR PARTNERS

These are your platform's foundation. Without them, you have no legitimacy and no traction.

What to do: Engage first. Co-design the platform with them. Give them visible leadership roles. In Morona Santiago, this was the Prefect's office (administrative authority plus convening power) and the Shuar Federation FICSH (territorial sovereignty plus community mobilization). These two organizations had to agree on the platform's structure before anyone else was invited.

### Quadrant II: High Authority, Low Influence -- FORMAL GATEKEEPERS

They can block your platform legally but cannot drive participation. They sign documents. They do not fill rooms.

What to do: Secure their formal endorsement early, but do not design the platform around them. In Orellana, the Ministry of Environment regional office had permitting authority over conservation zones, but no field presence and limited relationships with communities. We needed their signature on the constitutive agreement. We did not need them chairing sub-table meetings.

### Quadrant III: Low Authority, High Influence -- SHADOW POWER

They cannot sign agreements or allocate budgets, but they determine whether your platform has credibility on the ground. Ignore them and your platform is technically legitimate but practically dead.

What to do: Give them meaningful roles in execution, not just representation. In Sucumbios, a local NGO with no formal authority had deeper community trust than the provincial government. They became the bridge between the platform and communities that would otherwise never participate. Community-level leaders, elder councils, and women's organizations often sit in this quadrant. Their influence is invisible in org charts and decisive in practice.

### Quadrant IV: Low Authority, Low Influence -- PERIPHERAL PARTICIPANTS

They are interested but not essential. Including too many of them bloats your platform and slows decision-making.

What to do: Include them in information-sharing channels and plenary sessions. Do not give them seats on the coordination core. A platform with 50 organizations is a conference, not a governance body. 18-25 is the operational sweet spot. Most of the cut happens in this quadrant.

## How to Identify Must-Haves vs. Nice-to-Haves

Apply three tests to every organization on your map.

**Test 1: The Veto Test.** Can this organization block a critical process? Indigenous nations can invoke FPIC and halt any activity on their territory. The provincial government can refuse to sign the constitutive agreement. The Ministry of Environment can deny permits. If an organization has veto power over any process your platform depends on, they are a must-have. Period.

**Test 2: The Legitimacy Test.** If this organization is absent, will other key stakeholders question the platform's credibility? In the Amazon, a conservation platform without indigenous participation is not a conservation platform. It is a government meeting with NGO guests. A platform without the provincial government is an NGO coordination space with no institutional authority. Ask yourself: if this organization publicly says "we were not included," does that damage the platform?

**Test 3: The Execution Test.** Does this organization control resources, data, or capacity that the platform cannot replicate? If Conservation International exits, who has the monitoring systems? If the university leaves, who has the baseline data? If the agricultural cooperative is not at the table, who represents 2,000 smallholder families?

If an organization passes any one of these tests, they are a must-have. If they pass none, they are nice-to-have. In practice, must-haves number between 10 and 15 organizations. The remaining 8-10 seats go to nice-to-haves that add value without adding veto points.

## Three Power Dynamics That Will Surprise You

These are patterns I encountered repeatedly across all three provinces. They are not edge cases. They are structural features of multi-stakeholder conservation platforms in territories where the state, indigenous nations, and international organizations all have overlapping claims.

### 1. The Organization With the Least Formal Authority Often Controls the Room

In Morona Santiago, the Shuar Federation FICSH did not have the legal authority to approve provincial budgets or issue environmental permits. But when FICSH's president spoke, the room listened. When FICSH declined to participate in a sub-table session, that session lost its legitimacy. This is because FICSH represented territorial sovereignty over the majority of the province's land area. The provincial government administered the territory. FICSH governed it in the eyes of the communities who lived there. Your power map must capture this distinction or it is useless.

### 2. The Person Who Controls Information Controls the Agenda

Whoever presents the data at a Cooperation Table meeting shapes the conversation. In Orellana, the organization that produced the quarterly deforestation analysis set the agenda for the environmental sub-table, whether or not they had formal authority to do so. We learned to rotate data presentation responsibilities across organizations. When one NGO consistently produced the only available analysis, they had de facto agenda-setting power that was never formally granted. Watch for this. It is invisible in stakeholder lists and decisive in practice.

### 3. Bilateral Relationships Between Stakeholders Matter More Than Your Platform's Structure

The relationship between the Prefect and the indigenous federation president often determined whether the Cooperation Table functioned, regardless of what the constitutive agreement said. When those two had a working relationship, the platform ran smoothly. When provincial elections changed the Prefect, the platform stalled until a new bilateral relationship was established. Map the relationships between your key stakeholders, not just the stakeholders themselves. A platform where the two most powerful organizations distrust each other will fail no matter how well you design the governance architecture.

## The Mapping Process

### Step 1: Build the Initial Roster (Days 1-3)

Start with publicly available information. Pull the list of organizations operating in your territory from three sources: the provincial development plan (PDOT), the most recent donor-funded project's stakeholder annex, and the indigenous territorial governance plan (Plan de Vida) if one exists. This gives you a list of 40-60 organizations across the five sectors.

### Step 2: Conduct Key Informant Interviews (Days 4-10)

Interview 8-12 people from across all five sectors. Not organizational leaders. Interview the operational staff, the people who attend meetings and do the work. They will tell you things the directors will not.

Questions to ask:
- "When a decision needs to happen in this territory, which 3 organizations must agree?"
- "Which organization, if they refused to participate, would make any initiative lose credibility?"
- "Who actually shows up to meetings? Who sends a delegate? Who stops coming after the second session?"
- "Which organizations have a working relationship? Which ones are in open or quiet conflict?"
- "If money arrives for a new conservation initiative, who decides how it gets spent?"

### Step 3: Map the Four Dimensions (Days 11-12)

For each organization on your roster, score their position on each of the four power dimensions (Administrative Authority, Territorial Legitimacy, Technical Capacity, Financial Leverage) on a simple 1-3 scale. 1 means minimal. 3 means dominant. Do not overthink the scores. The point is relative positioning, not precision.

### Step 4: Plot the Canvas (Day 13)

Place each organization on the two-axis canvas (Formal Authority vs. Practical Influence). Use the four-dimension scores to inform placement. An organization with high Administrative Authority and Financial Leverage but low Territorial Legitimacy goes in Quadrant II (Formal Gatekeeper). An organization with high Territorial Legitimacy and Technical Capacity but low Administrative Authority goes in Quadrant III (Shadow Power).

### Step 5: Apply the Three Tests (Day 14)

Run every organization through the Veto Test, Legitimacy Test, and Execution Test. Separate must-haves from nice-to-haves. Your final invitation list should land between 18 and 25 organizations.

### Step 6: Validate With Two Trusted Insiders (Days 15-16)

Share your completed map with one person from the public sector and one from the indigenous authority sector. Ask them: "Who is missing? Who is placed wrong? What relationship did I miss?" These two validators will catch your blind spots. Choose people who have been in the territory for at least 5 years.

## Start Here

If you are about to launch a multi-stakeholder conservation platform, do the power map before anything else. Before you draft terms of reference. Before you write the first invitation. Before you book the meeting room.

The 16 days outlined in the mapping process above will save you 6 months of political repair work. I learned that the hard way in Morona Santiago, where a poorly mapped stakeholder landscape led to a first meeting that excluded a key indigenous federation, and the next two months were spent rebuilding trust that should never have been broken.
