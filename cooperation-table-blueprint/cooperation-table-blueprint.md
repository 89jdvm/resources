# The Cooperation Table Blueprint

## A Replication Guide for Multi-Stakeholder Conservation Platforms

**By Juan Diego Villacís** | Practitioner-Scholar for AI + Amazonian Governance

I built 3 Cooperation Tables for Conservation and Sustainable Development across the Ecuadorian Amazon. Morona Santiago with the GAD Provincial in 2024. Orellana and Sucumbíos with Conservation International in 2025. The Sucumbios table launched on July 30, 2025, with 50 representatives across 3 strategic axes in a single workshop. Each table coordinates 22-23 organizations across 5 sectors around a shared multi-year roadmap. This guide documents the exact process so you can replicate it.

---

## Who This Is For

You are a program manager at an INGO trying to coordinate multiple actors in a territory. Or a government official who inherited a province full of NGOs doing overlapping work. Or a DFI program officer wondering why your grantees in the same region never talk to each other. Or a freelance consultant hired to "build a coordination mechanism" with no template for what that actually means.

You need a multi-stakeholder platform that produces coordinated execution, not just meetings. This guide gives you the architecture, the sequence, and the operational details to build one.

---

## The Model in Brief

A Cooperation Table is a formal multi-stakeholder platform that brings 22-23 organizations from 5 sectors (public sector, indigenous authorities, NGOs, academia, private sector) into a single governance structure. Participants sign a constitutive agreement committing to a shared Hoja de Ruta (roadmap) with quarterly targets, organized into thematic sub-tables. Accountability comes from automated notifications, a public dashboard, and an escalation protocol. The platform is institutionalized through legal instruments (prefectoral decrees, formal agreements) so it survives political transitions and funding cycles.

---

## Phase 1: Political Foundation

### The platform lives or dies based on who convenes it.

Your first move is not stakeholder mapping. It is not a participatory workshop. It is a political calculation: who has the convening authority to put 22 organizations in a room and keep them coming back?

In Morona Santiago, the answer was the GAD Provincial (provincial government). The prefect's office had the administrative mandate over the territory and the political incentive to coordinate development actors. In Orellana and Sucumbíos, the convener was Conservation International, but the provincial government was brought in as co-convener from day one. Without government buy-in, the table lacks regulatory leverage and administrative continuity.

### Securing the Mandate

You need a formal mandate before you convene anyone. This means a written directive, an official letter, or an executive resolution authorizing the creation of the platform. In Morona Santiago, this came as a prefectoral commitment that later became a formal decree. In Orellana and Sucumbíos, the mandate came through Conservation International's programmatic authority, backed by co-convening letters from provincial governments.

Do not skip this step. A Cooperation Table convened informally ("let's all get together and coordinate") will collapse by month 4.

### Identifying Your Champion

Every successful table had one senior official who personally drove the process. Not the institution. A specific person with enough authority to clear bureaucratic obstacles, enough credibility to bring indigenous authorities to the table, and enough political interest to sustain effort over 8-12 months of negotiation and setup. In Morona Santiago, this was the prefect's office. Find your equivalent. If no one at the senior level cares enough to stake personal reputation on this platform, you are not ready to build it.

## Phase 2: Stakeholder Identification and Convening

### The Five Sectors

Every Cooperation Table I built drew from the same 5 stakeholder categories. This is not arbitrary. Each category provides a different type of legitimacy that the platform needs to function.

| Sector | What They Bring | Typical Count |
|--------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Public Sector** | Administrative authority, budget allocation, regulatory power. Provincial government, line ministries (environment, agriculture, planning), decentralized agencies. | 5-6 organizations |
| **Indigenous Authorities** | Territorial sovereignty, FPIC rights, historical legitimacy, community access. Federations, nationalities, self-governance structures. | 3-5 organizations |
| **Civil Society / NGOs** | Technical capacity, donor relationships, implementation experience. International and local conservation and development organizations. | 6-8 organizations |
| **Academia** | Data, research capacity, monitoring expertise. Universities and research centers operating in the territory. | 2-3 organizations |
| **Private Sector** | Economic activity, supply chain influence, employment. Companies, cooperatives, producer associations. | 3-4 organizations |

### How to Select

Start with a comprehensive scan: every organization operating in the territory with a mandate related to conservation, sustainable development, or territorial governance. In Orellana, this initial scan yielded over 40 organizations. The filter is operational relevance: does this organization make decisions, implement projects, or control resources that affect the territory? If the answer is no, they are an observer, not a member.

### The Right Number

The operational sweet spot is 22-23 organizations. Below 18, the platform lacks enough sectoral coverage to claim territorial legitimacy. Above 28, decision-making slows to a crawl and meetings become performative. All three of my tables landed in this range organically once the relevance filter was applied.

### Convening Sequence

Do not send a mass invitation for the first meeting. Convene in sequence:

1. **Government first.** Secure their co-ownership before anyone else is in the room.
2. **Indigenous authorities second.** Meet on their terms, in their territory if possible. Frame the platform as an extension of their governance, not a competing structure.
3. **Major NGOs third.** They have the implementation capacity and the donor relationships. Get their buy-in on the roadmap concept before the first plenary.
4. **Academia and private sector last.** They fill critical roles but rarely drive the process.

The first plenary session should feel like a formalization of agreements already reached, not a cold start.

## Phase 3: The Constitutive Agreement

### What It Is

The constitutive agreement is the founding document of the Cooperation Table. It is not an MoU. It is not a letter of intent. It is a formal agreement signed by the authorized representative of every member organization, establishing the platform's purpose, structure, governance rules, and commitments.

### What It Contains

At minimum, the constitutive agreement must include:

- **Name and scope.** The official name of the platform and the territory it covers.
- **Objective.** One paragraph. What specific coordination problem this platform solves.
- **Member list.** Every organization, with the name and title of the authorized signatory.
- **Governance structure.** How decisions are made. Plenary assemblies, sub-tables, coordination core. Voting protocols (consensus is standard for these platforms).
- **Commitment to the Hoja de Ruta.** Explicit agreement that all members will participate in building and executing the shared roadmap.
- **Meeting schedule.** Frequency and format of plenaries, sub-tables, and coordination meetings.
- **Duration.** Aligned with the roadmap time horizon (our tables: 2026-2028).
- **Modification clause.** How new members join, how the agreement is amended.

### Who Signs

The person who signs must have institutional authority to commit their organization. This means the prefect or vice-prefect (not the planning director), the federation president (not a technical officer), the NGO country director (not a project coordinator). Getting the right signatory level is half the political work of Phase 1 and Phase 2.

### Why It Matters

A signed constitutive agreement creates three forms of accountability: legal (the document can be referenced in administrative and institutional processes), social (no signatory wants to be publicly identified as the organization that defected from a 22-organization commitment), and operational (it provides the basis for tracking execution against the roadmap).

In Morona Santiago, 22 organizations signed. That collective signature created enough institutional gravity that the platform survived a political transition at the provincial level and produced the first 7 Conservation Agreements (signed June 27, 2025), delivering solar internet, energy kits, and water purification to Shuar and Achuar communities. The constitutive agreement made those agreements possible. Without the governance structure, no single organization could have brokered them.

## Phase 4: Building the Hoja de Ruta

### What the Hoja de Ruta Is

The Hoja de Ruta (roadmap) is the execution instrument of the Cooperation Table. It translates the platform's purpose into specific, time-bound, organization-assigned activities. Without it, the Cooperation Table is a meeting. With it, the Cooperation Table is a governance mechanism.

### Roadmap Design

The roadmap is organized into thematic sub-tables. Each sub-table covers a distinct area of work relevant to the territory's conservation and development priorities.

In Orellana, we built 3 sub-tables:
- Ambiente (Environment)
- Social e Infraestructura (Social and Infrastructure)
- Desarrollo Económico (Economic Development)

In Sucumbíos, we built 5 sub-tables:
- Ambiental (Environmental)
- Agroproductivo (Agroproductive)
- Patrimonio (Heritage)
- Social
- Gobernanza (Governance)

The number of sub-tables depends on the territory's complexity and the number of active thematic areas. Three is the minimum for meaningful coverage. Five is manageable. Above six, coordination overhead between sub-tables becomes a problem.

### Activity Structure

Each activity in the roadmap has 6 required fields:

1. **Description.** Specific and measurable. "Establish 3 community monitoring stations in the buffer zone of Yasuní by Q2 2027." Not "promote environmental monitoring."
2. **Responsible organization(s).** Named. Not "the platform" or "to be determined."
3. **Sub-table.** Which thematic group owns this activity.
4. **Timeline.** Quarterly milestones within the 2026-2028 horizon.
5. **Status.** Pending, In Progress, Completed, Delayed.
6. **Dependencies.** What must happen before this activity can start.

### How Many Activities

Orellana's roadmap has 79 activities. Sucumbíos has 93. The difference reflects Sucumbíos's additional sub-tables and the greater organizational density in the province. A roadmap with fewer than 40 activities is probably too vague. A roadmap with more than 120 is probably listing tasks, not strategic activities.

### How to Build It

Do not build the roadmap in a plenary. Build it in sub-table working sessions where the 6-10 organizations with relevant expertise propose, debate, and assign activities. Then bring the compiled roadmap to a plenary for validation and formal adoption.

The building process takes 2-3 months of sub-table sessions. Do not rush it. A roadmap that organizations helped build is a roadmap they will execute. A roadmap handed to them by a consultant is a document they will ignore.

## Phase 5: Accountability Infrastructure

### The Fundamental Problem

22 organizations committed to 79-93 activities over 3 years. Without tracking infrastructure, you will discover non-compliance at the annual review. By then, it is too late to recover the timeline.

### Three Components

**1. The Progress Dashboard**

A visual tracking system that shows the status of every activity by province, sub-table, and quarter. Every stakeholder can see, at a glance, which activities are on track and which are delayed. The dashboard eliminates the ability of any organization to quietly drop a commitment. Public visibility is the strongest compliance mechanism in multi-stakeholder platforms.

**2. The Notification Engine**

Automated email reminders sent to the responsible organization as activity deadlines approach. Reminder at 30 days before deadline. Second reminder at 7 days. Escalation notice when the deadline passes. This system replaces the manual follow-up that burns out coordination staff and creates political friction ("why are you singling us out?"). The system sends the same notifications to everyone, based on the same rules.

**3. The Escalation Protocol**

When an activity is overdue, the sequence is:
1. Automated notification to the responsible organization.
2. Flagging in the dashboard (visible to all stakeholders).
3. Discussion in the next sub-table session.
4. If unresolved, escalation to the coordination core.
5. If still unresolved, formal communication and reporting in the plenary.

No penalties need to be punitive. Social accountability among 22 peer organizations is powerful enough. The goal is to make non-compliance visible, not to punish it.

### Build vs. Buy

You can build this infrastructure with standard tools. A structured spreadsheet or database for the roadmap data. An email automation layer for notifications. A dashboard tool (Google Sheets, Power BI, or a custom solution) for visualization. Assign one person (a technical coordinator or secretariat staff member) the explicit responsibility of updating activity status monthly. In our experience, when that person was pulled to another project for 6 weeks, the dashboard went stale and two sub-tables stopped checking it. Dedicated data maintenance is a staffing decision, not a technology decision.

## Phase 6: Institutionalization

### The Problem

Every multi-stakeholder platform I have seen die was killed by the same thing: the project that funded it ended, the political administration changed, or the champion moved on. If the platform has no institutional life independent of its founding conditions, it will not survive.

### The Morona Santiago Model

The Cooperation Table in Morona Santiago was institutionalized through a prefectoral decree. This is a formal legal instrument issued by the provincial prefect that establishes the platform as a permanent function of the provincial government. The decree means:

- The platform is part of the provincial government's organizational structure, not a project component.
- Budget allocation for platform coordination can be included in the provincial annual budget.
- A new prefect cannot dissolve the platform without issuing a new decree (a politically costly move if 22 organizations are invested).
- The platform has legal identity for the purpose of interinstitutional agreements.

This is the gold standard for institutionalization at the subnational level in Ecuador. The equivalent in other countries would be a gubernatorial executive order, a provincial ordinance, or a resolution from the regional assembly.

### When the Decree Is Not Possible

In Orellana and Sucumbíos, the tables were convened under Conservation International's programmatic authority rather than government decree. The institutionalization strategy is different: the constitutive agreement itself, signed by 22-23 organizations, creates a web of institutional commitments that is difficult to dissolve unilaterally. The goal is to transition platform coordination to a permanent institution (the provincial government or a regional body) before Conservation International's contract ends.

### Institutionalization Checklist

- [ ] Constitutive agreement signed by all member organizations
- [ ] Roadmap formally adopted as a shared execution instrument
- [ ] Platform referenced in at least one government planning document (provincial development plan, annual operating plan)
- [ ] Coordination function assigned to a permanent institution (not the project team)
- [ ] Legal instrument (decree, ordinance, resolution) establishing the platform as a permanent body
- [ ] Multi-funder or co-financing structure to avoid single-donor dependency

### Timing

Do not wait until the project's final year to pursue institutionalization. In Morona Santiago, the decree was pursued from the first year. Start the legal and political groundwork in Phase 1 and maintain it in parallel with Phases 2-5.

## What Will Go Wrong

I have built three of these. Every one of them hit problems. Here is what to expect and how to handle it.

**The government will try to control the agenda.** Provincial governments often see the Cooperation Table as "their" platform, not a multi-stakeholder space. They will push to chair every meeting, filter communications, and sideline organizations that challenge them. Counter: establish co-governance from the constitutive agreement. Rotating facilitation. Meeting minutes distributed by the secretariat, not the government.

**Indigenous authorities will be skeptical.** They have been invited to hundreds of "coordination" meetings that produced nothing. They will question why this time is different. They will also resist any structure that positions them as "one of five sectors" when they hold territorial sovereignty. Counter: meet with indigenous leadership separately before the first plenary. Acknowledge their governance structures (life plans, consultative assemblies) as co-equal inputs, not subordinate to the platform.

**NGOs will protect their turf.** Organizations with donor-funded projects in the territory will resist coordinating if they see the table as a threat to their autonomy or donor relationships. Counter: frame the table as a visibility mechanism, not a control mechanism. Coordination through the roadmap increases their reporting value to donors, not decreases it.

**Activity status will go stale.** Even with automated notifications, some organizations will not update their activity status for months. The dashboard will show misleading data. Counter: the monthly update is a fixed responsibility of the technical coordinator. Use sub-table sessions to validate status in person.

**Political transitions will test everything.** Elections happen. Prefects change. New officials arrive with no institutional memory of the Cooperation Table. Counter: this is why the decree matters. It is also why the constitutive agreement must be signed at the institutional level, not the personal level. When the new official arrives, 21 other organizations are still committed. The social cost of abandoning the platform is higher than the cost of continuing it.

**Attendance will drop after month 6.** The first 2-3 meetings are well-attended because they are novel. By month 6, only the committed organizations show up. Counter: sub-tables with focused agendas maintain engagement better than plenaries with generic updates. Only call plenaries when there are real decisions to make.
