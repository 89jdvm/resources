# Direct Funding Readiness Score

## Can Your Indigenous Fund Bypass the INGO Middleman?

**By Juan Diego Villacís**

At COP26, donors pledged $1.7 billion for indigenous and local community forest tenure. By 2024, the Rainforest Foundation Norway estimated less than 7% reached indigenous organizations directly. The rest flows through international NGOs who take a management fee, impose their reporting templates, and control the disbursement timeline. Indigenous organizations end up as sub-awardees on their own territorial projects.

It is a capacity gap. It is fixable.

I coordinated with ~30 indigenous nations across Ecuador and Peru through the Sacred Headwaters Alliance. I ran Fundación IKIAM as Executive Director, securing direct grants for the Achuar Nation. I have seen what passes and what fails when an indigenous organization applies for direct DFI funding.

This assessment identifies the specific gaps. Fifteen questions across five dimensions. Score yourself honestly. The gaps you find are the gaps keeping you in sub-awardee status.

---

## How the Assessment Works

Each question scores 0, 1, or 2 points. Maximum score: 30 points.

- **24-30: Ready for direct funding.** You can apply as prime contractor to most DFI mechanisms. Focus on relationship building and proposal quality.
- **16-23: Close but not there.** You have the fundamentals. Specific gaps need closing. This assessment tells you which ones.
- **8-15: Significant gaps.** Direct funding is 12-18 months away with focused capacity building.
- **0-7: Early stage.** Begin with smaller bilateral grants and build systems incrementally.

---

## Dimension 1: Financial Management Systems (6 points)

### Q1. Does your organization use dedicated accounting software with an auditable trail?

- **2 points:** Yes. We use software (QuickBooks, Wave, Xero, or equivalent) with transaction logs, categories, and exportable reports.
- **1 point:** We use Excel spreadsheets with consistent formatting and backup copies.
- **0 points:** Financial records are in paper notebooks or inconsistent digital files.

Why this matters: USAID's Organizational Capacity Assessment (OCA) requires demonstrable accounting systems. "We keep track of expenses" is not sufficient. The auditor needs to see a trail from receipt to ledger to report.

### Q2. Has your organization been independently audited in the last 24 months?

- **2 points:** Yes. We have a clean audit opinion from a recognized firm.
- **1 point:** We have been audited but with qualifications or management letter findings.
- **0 points:** We have never been independently audited.

Why this matters: Most DFIs require audited financial statements as a pre-condition for eligibility. The audit itself is less important than what it reveals about your internal controls.

### Q3. Do you have a written financial policies and procedures manual?

- **2 points:** Yes. It covers procurement, disbursement, expense approval thresholds, and conflict of interest. Staff have been trained on it.
- **1 point:** We have some written policies but they are incomplete or not consistently followed.
- **0 points:** Financial procedures are informal and undocumented.

Why this matters: DFIs will ask for this document during due diligence. It does not need to be 100 pages. It needs to be real, specific to your organization, and demonstrably in use.

---

## Dimension 2: Governance and Institutional Structure (6 points)

### Q4. Does your organization have a legally constituted board or governing body with documented meeting records?

- **2 points:** Yes. The board meets at minimum quarterly. Meeting minutes are recorded and filed.
- **1 point:** We have a governing body but meetings are irregular or not documented.
- **0 points:** Governance is informal with no documented decision-making records.

Why this matters: DFIs need to see that decisions are made by a body, not by an individual. The documented meeting trail proves collective governance. For indigenous organizations, this can align with existing governance structures (consultative assemblies, congresses) as long as they are documented.

### Q5. Are roles, responsibilities, and authority levels clearly defined in writing?

- **2 points:** Yes. We have an organizational chart, job descriptions, and documented signing authorities (who can authorize payments at what thresholds).
- **1 point:** Roles are understood but not formally documented.
- **0 points:** Roles are fluid and undocumented.

Why this matters: When USAID or GEF conducts a pre-award assessment, they look for segregation of duties. The person who approves a purchase should not be the same person who makes the payment. If you cannot show this in writing, you fail the assessment.

### Q6. Does your organization have a conflict of interest policy?

- **2 points:** Yes. Written, signed by all board members and senior staff, with procedures for disclosure and recusal.
- **1 point:** We have an informal understanding but no written policy.
- **0 points:** No policy exists.

Why this matters: This is one of the easiest boxes to check and one of the most commonly missed. DFIs will not fund organizations where board members can direct funds to their own businesses without a documented disclosure process.

---

## Dimension 3: Project Management and Reporting (6 points)

### Q7. Has your organization successfully managed and reported on an externally funded project of $50,000 or more?

- **2 points:** Yes. We delivered final programmatic and financial reports that were accepted by the funder.
- **1 point:** We have managed smaller grants (under $50K) with final reporting.
- **0 points:** We have not managed externally funded projects with formal reporting requirements.

Why this matters: Track record is the strongest predictor of future performance in every DFI's evaluation framework. A successfully closed $50K grant is worth more than a $500K letter of intent. Start small, close clean, build the record.

### Q8. Do you have a monitoring and evaluation (M&E) system for tracking project indicators?

- **2 points:** Yes. We collect data against defined indicators, analyze it, and use it to adjust implementation.
- **1 point:** We collect data but analysis and use for decision-making is inconsistent.
- **0 points:** We report on activities completed but do not systematically track indicators.

Why this matters: GEF independent evaluations consistently cite M&E design shortcomings as the primary driver of project underperformance. DFIs want to see that you measure outcomes, not just outputs. "We conducted 12 workshops" is an output. "85% of workshop participants adopted the financial tool within 6 months" is an outcome.

### Q9. Can your organization produce a narrative and financial report in English or the funder's required language?

- **2 points:** Yes. We have staff or contracted capacity to produce donor-quality reports in English (or French/Spanish as required).
- **1 point:** We can produce reports in Spanish but need external support for English.
- **0 points:** We do not have report-writing capacity in the funder's required language.

Why this matters: Language is a barrier that is solvable. AI translation tools have dramatically reduced this gap. But you need someone who can review the translation for technical accuracy and donor-specific terminology.

---

## Dimension 4: Human Resources and Operational Capacity (6 points)

### Q10. Does your organization have at least one full-time staff member dedicated to financial management?

- **2 points:** Yes. A trained accountant or finance officer who is not also the program director.
- **1 point:** Financial management is handled by a staff member with other primary responsibilities.
- **0 points:** Financial management is handled by the director or board members directly.

Why this matters: DFI assessors look for segregation of duties. If your Executive Director also manages the checkbook, the assessor flags this as a single-point-of-failure risk. A dedicated finance person, even part-time, solves this.

### Q11. Does your organization have a physical office, internet connectivity, and basic IT infrastructure?

- **2 points:** Yes. Office, reliable internet (even if intermittent), computers, and secure file storage.
- **1 point:** We have an office but connectivity and IT infrastructure are unreliable.
- **0 points:** We operate from personal residences or community spaces without dedicated infrastructure.

Why this matters: DFIs will ask where financial records are stored and how they are backed up. "On the director's personal laptop" fails the assessment. An institutional office with a shared drive or cloud backup passes it.

### Q12. Do you have a documented procurement process for purchasing goods and services?

- **2 points:** Yes. Written policy with thresholds (e.g., 3 quotes required above $500, competitive bidding above $5,000).
- **1 point:** We get quotes but the process is not documented as a formal policy.
- **0 points:** Procurement is done informally.

Why this matters: Procurement violations are the number one audit finding in USAID-funded projects globally. A documented procurement policy with clear thresholds is the single most protective institutional document you can have.

---

## Dimension 5: Legal and Compliance Framework (6 points)

### Q13. Is your organization registered as a legal entity with tax-exempt or non-profit status?

- **2 points:** Yes. Legally constituted, registered with the relevant government authority, with current tax filings.
- **1 point:** Registered but tax filings are not current or status is under review.
- **0 points:** Not formally registered or operating under another organization's legal identity.

### Q14. Does your organization have a bank account in its own name with multi-signatory controls?

- **2 points:** Yes. Institutional bank account requiring two signatures for withdrawals above a defined threshold.
- **1 point:** We have a bank account but with single-signatory access.
- **0 points:** We use personal bank accounts or cash management.

### Q15. Does your organization have documented policies for anti-fraud, anti-corruption, and whistleblower protection?

- **2 points:** Yes. Written policies, accessible to all staff, with a designated reporting channel.
- **1 point:** Some policies exist but they are incomplete or not communicated.
- **0 points:** No formal anti-fraud or anti-corruption policies.

## Interpreting Your Score

Add up your points across all 15 questions.

**24-30 points: Ready.**
Your organization has the institutional architecture to apply for direct DFI funding. Your limiting factor is not capacity. It is visibility, relationships, and proposal quality. Focus on the DFI Decoder guide and building direct relationships with program officers.

**16-23 points: Close.**
You have the foundation. The gaps you identified are specific and closable. Most can be addressed in 6-12 months with targeted work. Prioritize: financial policies manual, independent audit, procurement documentation, and at least one successfully closed small grant.

**8-15 points: Building.**
Direct funding is 12-18 months away. Work with a fiscal sponsor or INGO partner for current projects while building internal systems. Focus on: accounting software, governance documentation, and closing one small grant ($10-50K) with clean reporting.

**0-7 points: Starting.**
Begin with bilateral grants under $25K that have lighter reporting requirements. Build the track record. Every clean close adds credibility. Do not attempt major DFI applications at this stage.

## The Honest Part

This assessment is a diagnostic, not a judgment. Many of the most impactful organizations on the planet would score below 15 on this tool. That does not mean they are not effective. It means DFI bureaucracy was not designed for them.

The path from sub-awardee to direct recipient is not about becoming more "professional." It is about building the specific institutional scaffolding that DFIs require in order to transfer fiduciary responsibility. Nothing more. Nothing less.
