# The First 90 Days

## Week-by-Week Playbook for Launching a Multi-Stakeholder Governance Platform

**By Juan Diego Villacís**, Practitioner-Scholar for AI + Amazonian Governance

I launched 3 Cooperation Tables for Conservation and Sustainable Development in the Ecuadorian Amazon. Each one brought together 22-23 organizations across public sector, indigenous authorities, NGOs, academia, and private sector. Each one produced a constitutive agreement and a multi-year execution roadmap (hoja de ruta) with 79-93 named activities.

This playbook is the operational sequence I followed. Not the theory. The actual week-by-week choreography of who to call, what to ask them, what to avoid saying, and what to do when things break.

The hardest part of building a multi-stakeholder platform is not the first meeting. It is getting organizations to show up for the second meeting when nothing from the first one got done. This playbook is designed around that problem.

---

## Before Day 1: The Pre-Launch Checklist

Do not start the 90-day clock until every item on this list is in place. Starting without them means you will burn your first impression with stakeholders on logistics failures.

**Institutional anchor confirmed.** You need one institution willing to formally convene the process. In my case, this was the provincial government (prefectura). The convener does not need to fund everything. They need to lend their letterhead and their political legitimacy. If no institution will put their name on the invitation, you are not ready.

**Political mapping completed.** You need to know, before your first phone call, the current political dynamics between the prefect's office and the indigenous federations. Between the prefect and the municipal mayors. Between the largest NGOs and the government. If there is an active conflict between two organizations you plan to seat at the same table, you need a strategy for it. Not a hope. A strategy.

**Stakeholder long list drafted.** Write down every organization that operates in the territory, organized by the five categories: public sector, indigenous authorities, NGOs, academia, private sector. For each one, name the person who makes decisions about participation. Not the communications officer. Not the junior program assistant. The person who can say "yes, we will commit staff time and resources to this."

**Budget for 90 days secured.** You need meeting logistics (venue, travel support for remote communities, meals), a coordinator's time (at least half-time), and communication materials (a one-page concept note, an invitation letter). If you are waiting on a grant decision, do not start.

**Concept note written.** One page maximum. The problem. The proposed structure. Who is being invited. What participation means. What it does NOT mean (this matters more than you think). No jargon. Written so a canton mayor and a federation president can both read it and see their role.

## Weeks 1-2: Political Groundwork

This is the most important two weeks of the entire process. You are not organizing logistics. You are building the political coalition that will make or break the platform.

### Who to approach first

The order matters. Get it wrong and you create the perception that one group owns the process.

**Week 1, Day 1-2: The provincial government (prefect or vice-prefect).** This is a courtesy call and a negotiation. You need their formal backing, their letterhead, and ideally a venue for the first convening. In return, they get to be seen as the convener of a major governance initiative. Be explicit about what this is and what it is not. The prefect's office will want to control the platform. You must establish from day one that the platform is multi-stakeholder, not governmental. Use this language: "The prefectura convenes. The platform governs collectively." Get the prefect's written endorsement of the concept note before you approach anyone else.

**Week 1, Day 3-4: Indigenous authorities (federation presidents, nationality leaders).** This is the conversation that determines whether you build a governance platform or a government advisory board. Indigenous authorities need to see this platform as an extension of their existing governance, not a competing structure. Bring their life plans (planes de vida). Reference their existing governance assemblies. Show them where their priorities appear in the concept note. Ask them what would need to be true for this to strengthen, rather than dilute, their authority. If they say they need to consult with their bases before committing, respect that timeline completely. Do not pressure them. They are testing whether you will steamroll their process later.

**Week 1, Day 5 through Week 2:** The remaining stakeholders, in this order:

1. **Lead NGOs operating in the territory** (the ones with budget and staff on the ground). Meet with country directors or program directors, not project coordinators. Warn them now: this platform requires institutional commitment, not observer status. If they send junior staff to the first meeting, the government will notice and deprioritize them.

2. **Academic institutions** (universities, research centers). They are usually the easiest yes because participation costs them little and gives them field access. But pin them down on what they will contribute: data, monitoring capacity, student fieldwork, technical analysis. "We support this initiative" is not a commitment.

3. **Key municipal governments** (the 2-3 cantons where the most activity is concentrated). They are often suspicious that the provincial government is encroaching on their jurisdiction. Frame the platform as coordination, not hierarchy. Explain that cantonal priorities will have their own space in the roadmap.

4. **Private sector** (the hardest). Start with whichever company has the most to gain or the most to lose from their reputation in the territory. Do not lead with "corporate social responsibility." Lead with operational risk, regulatory exposure, or supply chain requirements. If they see the platform as a CSR checkbox they will send their communications department and you will never get a real commitment.

### What to ask in every meeting

Three questions, in this order:

1. "What is the biggest coordination gap you face in this territory right now?" Listen. Write it down. You will use their exact words in the roadmap later.
2. "What would this platform need to look like for your organization to participate at a decision-making level?" This surfaces their conditions and red lines early.
3. "Who from your organization would attend regularly, and at what level of authority?" If they name someone without decision-making authority, push back gently: "For this to work, we need someone who can commit resources on behalf of your organization in the room."

### What to avoid saying

Do not promise outcomes. Do not promise funding. Do not say "this will solve" anything. Say: "This platform will create the structure for coordinated action. What gets done depends on what each organization commits to."

## Weeks 3-4: First Convening

You have one shot at the first plenary. If it feels like a waste of time, half the room will not come back.

### Meeting design (not agenda, design)

**Venue.** Neutral ground. Not the prefect's office (signals government control). Not an NGO conference room (signals donor project). A university auditorium or a cultural center works well. If the territory includes indigenous communities, rotating subsequent meetings into indigenous territory builds legitimacy.

**Duration.** One full day. Not half a day. Not two days. The first meeting needs enough time for every stakeholder category to speak, but not so much time that people leave early and miss the commitment session at the end.

**Seating.** Do not put stakeholders in audience rows facing a head table. Use a U-shape or a large circle. The spatial arrangement communicates power dynamics. If the prefect sits at a head table and everyone else faces them, you have built a government consultation, not a multi-stakeholder platform.

### The agenda (4 blocks)

**Block 1 (90 minutes): Territorial diagnosis.** Present the coordination gap data you gathered in Weeks 1-2. Use stakeholders' own words. "When we spoke with [federation president], they identified X as their top coordination challenge. The prefectura identified Y. The largest NGO identified Z." Show the overlap. This validates their input and demonstrates that the platform is responding to real needs, not an imported agenda.

**Block 2 (60 minutes): Proposed structure.** Present the Governance Canvas (if you have used that resource) or your own structural proposal. Keep it simple: purpose, membership, decision-making authority, meeting frequency, thematic sub-tables. Do NOT present the roadmap yet. That comes in Weeks 7-8. This meeting is about the architecture, not the execution plan.

**Block 3 (90 minutes): Stakeholder input on the structure.** Organize by sector. Give each sector 15 minutes to discuss among themselves what they need from the structure. Then each sector presents their conditions. Write them on a visible board. This is where you find out that the indigenous federation wants co-presidency, or that the prefect wants the technical secretariat housed in their planning department, or that the NGOs want observer status only. All of these are negotiable. But you need to surface them now, not in Week 6 when you are negotiating the constitutive agreement.

**Block 4 (60 minutes): Commitments and next steps.** Specific. Named. Dated. "Organization X will designate their representative by [date]. The constitutive agreement drafting committee will consist of [names]. The next meeting will be on [date] at [location]." If people leave without specific personal commitments, the second meeting will not happen.

### Who speaks and when

The prefect or vice-prefect opens. Brief. 5 minutes. Then immediately hand the floor to an indigenous authority. This signals co-governance from minute one. The rest of the opening should feature one voice from each sector, each under 5 minutes. Do not let any single speaker dominate the first hour.

### What to avoid

Do not allow the first meeting to become a series of institutional presentations where each organization describes their projects for 20 minutes. This is the single most common failure mode. If an organization starts doing this, the moderator must redirect: "Thank you. We will map all ongoing projects in the next phase. Right now we need to agree on the platform structure."

Do not allow the meeting to end without scheduling the next meeting. If you leave the date open, you lose momentum and the calendar becomes a negotiation.

## Weeks 5-6: Constitutive Agreement Negotiation

The constitutive agreement is the founding document of the platform. It formalizes who belongs, what the platform can do, how decisions are made, and how it sustains itself. This is where political interests collide with governance design.

### What goes into the constitutive agreement

1. **Name and purpose of the platform.** Straightforward, but expect debate on the name. Every sector wants the name to reflect their priority. "Cooperation Table for Conservation" excludes development actors. "Cooperation Table for Sustainable Development" makes conservation actors nervous. In our case, the full name included both: "Mesa de Cooperación para la Conservación y el Desarrollo Sostenible."

2. **Membership.** Which organizations are founding members. How new members join. How members exit or are removed.

3. **Governance structure.** Presidency or co-presidency. Technical secretariat. Sub-tables and their coordinators.

4. **Decision-making protocol.** Consensus is ideal but slow. Qualified majority (two-thirds) with veto rights for indigenous authorities on matters affecting their territories is the model that worked for us.

5. **Meeting frequency and format.** Plenary biannually. Sub-tables quarterly. Coordination core monthly.

6. **Commitments.** What each signatory organization commits to contribute (staff time, data, logistics, technical support).

7. **Duration and renewal.** How long the agreement is valid. How it gets renewed.

### What goes wrong

**Government wants the presidency.** The prefect's office will argue they have the administrative capacity to lead. They are right, and it does not matter. If the government holds the presidency alone, indigenous authorities and NGOs will see it as a government initiative and disengage. Push for co-presidency between the government and the indigenous authority. In Morona Santiago, this structure worked because it gave the Shuar federation equal standing with the prefectura in the governance of the platform.

**NGOs want observer status.** Large INGOs will try to participate without committing to the constitutive agreement. They want to attend, contribute opinions, and keep their flexibility. Do not allow this for organizations operating in the territory. If they have staff and budget deployed in the territory, they are stakeholders, not observers. Observer status is for organizations genuinely external to the territory (a national-level think tank, a donor agency monitoring their investment).

**Signature level negotiations.** The constitutive agreement is only as strong as the people who sign it. If the prefect sends a department head to sign instead of signing personally, every other organization will downgrade their signatory too. Hold firm: the agreement is signed by the highest authority of each organization. Prefect. Federation president. NGO country director. University rector. This creates personal political investment in the platform's success.

**Language negotiations.** In territories with indigenous languages, the agreement should exist in both Spanish and the relevant indigenous language(s). This is not symbolic. It communicates that the platform operates in a plurinational governance context.

### What to hold firm on

Three non-negotiables:

1. **Co-governance structure.** No single organization controls the platform.
2. **Named commitments.** Every signatory commits specific resources, not vague "support."
3. **Accountability clause.** The agreement includes a mechanism for reviewing compliance. Without this, the agreement is decorative.

### Timeline for this phase

Draft the agreement in Week 5. Circulate to all potential signatories. Collect feedback. In Week 6, hold a negotiation session (smaller group: 2-3 representatives per sector) to resolve disputes. Target signature at the beginning of Week 7, or at the latest, during the roadmap design workshops in Weeks 7-8.

## Weeks 7-8: Roadmap Design Workshops

This is where the platform shifts from architecture to execution. The roadmap (hoja de ruta) is the single document that every stakeholder will reference for the next 2-3 years. It must be built collectively, or it will be ignored collectively.

### How to run the workshops

**Organize by sub-table.** Do not try to build the entire roadmap in a single plenary session. Break into the thematic sub-tables defined in the constitutive agreement (e.g., Environmental, Agroproductive, Socio-Economic, Governance, Education). Each sub-table runs its own workshop with the 6-10 organizations relevant to that theme.

**Duration per sub-table workshop: half a day.** Longer sessions produce diminishing returns. Shorter sessions do not give enough time to move past generalities into specific activities.

**Workshop structure (4 hours):**

Hour 1: Present the territorial diagnosis relevant to this sub-table. What data exists. What gaps are known. What each organization is already doing in this thematic area. The goal is to make duplication and gaps visible.

Hour 2: Brainstorm activities. Each organization proposes activities that address the identified gaps. Write every proposal on cards or a shared digital board. Do not filter yet.

Hour 3: Filter and assign. For each proposed activity, ask three questions: (1) Is this specific enough to be tracked? "Promote environmental education" is not. "Train 50 community monitors in water quality sampling by Q3 2027" is. (2) Which organization takes lead responsibility? If no one volunteers, the activity is aspirational, not operational. Remove it. (3) What is the quarterly timeline?

Hour 4: Review the complete sub-table activity list. Identify dependencies between activities. Flag resource gaps. Each responsible organization verbally confirms their commitments in front of the group.

### How to get real commitments

The difference between a roadmap that gets executed and one that collects dust comes down to one moment: when you ask an organization to put their name next to an activity and a deadline in a room full of their peers.

This social accountability is the most powerful enforcement mechanism you have. It is stronger than any legal clause. When the WWF country director says "We will complete X by Q2 2027" in front of the prefect, the federation president, and 20 other organizations, that commitment carries weight.

To make this moment work:

- Ask organizations to confirm verbally, not just nod.
- Write the commitment on a visible board immediately.
- Read back the full list at the end of the workshop: organization, activity, deadline.
- Photograph the board and distribute it within 24 hours.

### Volume benchmarks

From our three platforms: the Orellana roadmap produced 79 activities across 4 sub-tables. Sucumbios produced 93 activities across 5 sub-tables. If your roadmap has fewer than 40 activities, you probably stayed too high-level. If it has more than 120, you have included items that should be internal organizational tasks, not platform-level coordination.

### Common failure

Organizations commit to activities they know they cannot fund. They assume funding will materialize. It will not. For every activity, ask: "Do you currently have the budget and staff to complete this, or does it require additional funding?" If additional funding is needed, note that explicitly in the roadmap and assign a parallel activity: "Submit proposal to [donor] for funding Activity X by Q1 2027." Unfunded activities without a funding strategy are not roadmap items. They are wishes.

## Weeks 9-10: Accountability Setup

The roadmap exists. Now build the system that makes it impossible to ignore.

### Assign tracking responsibilities

Designate a technical secretariat or coordination unit. This can be one person initially. Their job is not to execute activities. Their job is to know the status of every activity and to make that status visible to every stakeholder.

In our model, the technical secretariat consisted of one coordinator per province, supported by a monitoring and notification system. The coordinator's weekly routine:

- Monday: review the activity tracker for upcoming deadlines (next 30 days).
- Tuesday-Thursday: contact responsible organizations for status updates.
- Friday: update the master tracker and flag overdue items.

### Design the notification flow

Every responsible organization should receive automated or manual notifications at three intervals:

1. **30 days before deadline:** Reminder of the upcoming activity and deadline. Tone: informational. "Activity X is due in 30 days. Please confirm status."
2. **On the deadline:** Status request. "Activity X is due today. Please report: completed, in progress (with expected completion date), or delayed (with explanation)."
3. **15 days after a missed deadline:** Escalation notice. "Activity X is 15 days overdue. This will be reported to the coordination core and the next sub-table session." Copy the coordination core.

The three-notice system works because it establishes a predictable rhythm. Organizations learn that deadlines are tracked, that delays are visible, and that non-response triggers escalation. By the second quarter, most organizations start reporting proactively.

### Build the visibility layer

Status must be public. Not "available upon request." Public. Options:

- **Dashboard.** A simple web-based dashboard showing each activity's status (on track, delayed, completed) by sub-table, responsible organization, and quarter. This is what we built, and it changed the dynamic completely. When an organization can see that 18 of the 22 signatories are on track and they are one of the 4 that are not, the social pressure self-corrects.

- **Quarterly status report.** If a dashboard is not feasible, a PDF or spreadsheet distributed to all signatories before each sub-table meeting, showing every activity and its status. Color-coded: green, yellow, red.

- **WhatsApp group update.** Minimum viable option. Weekly status summary sent to the platform's WhatsApp group. This works in contexts where stakeholders check WhatsApp more reliably than email.

### What not to do

Do not make the coordinator the bottleneck for all information. Build the system so that responsible organizations update their own status directly (via a form, shared spreadsheet, or dashboard input). The coordinator verifies and follows up on non-reporters. If the coordinator has to chase every single organization every single week, they will burn out by Month 3.

## Weeks 11-12: First Progress Check

This is the moment of truth. Twelve weeks in, the constitutive agreement is signed, the roadmap is built, and the accountability system is running. Now you find out whether organizations actually did what they said they would do.

### What to measure

Pull a status report from your tracking system. Calculate these numbers:

- **Completion rate:** Of all activities due in Q1, how many are completed? Across our 3 tables, Q1 completion ranged from 38% (Sucumbíos, which had 93 activities and 5 sub-tables) to 52% (Morona Santiago, smaller scope). Below 30% signals structural problems in activity assignment. Above 60% usually means activities were defined too loosely.
- **Reporting rate:** Of all responsible organizations, how many reported their status without being chased? This is a proxy for institutional engagement. If fewer than half self-report, your notification system needs redesign.
- **Attendance persistence:** Compare attendance at the first convening (Weeks 3-4) with attendance at the first sub-table working sessions (Weeks 11-12). A 20-30% drop is normal. More than 40% drop means either the structure is not working or specific sectors are disengaging.
- **Sub-table activity:** Are all sub-tables active, or have one or two gone dormant? A dormant sub-table usually means the coordinator dropped the ball or the thematic area lost its champion.

### How to present it

Do not present the first progress check as a judgment. Present it as a baseline. "This is where we are after 12 weeks. Here is what is working. Here is what needs adjustment."

Prepare a one-page summary per sub-table:

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Activities due this quarter | X |
| Completed | X (Y%) |
| In progress | X (Y%) |
| Delayed | X (Y%) |
| Not reported | X (Y%) |
| Organizations reporting on time | X of Y |

Present this at the sub-table level first, not in a plenary. Give organizations the chance to explain delays to their 6-10 peers before exposing them in front of 22.

### What breaks at this stage

**Government staff turnover.** The official who attended the first convening has been reassigned. The new person has never heard of the platform and questions why their institution committed to activities they did not personally agree to. This is the single most destructive pattern in multi-stakeholder governance. Mitigation: the constitutive agreement should bind the institution, not the individual. When a new person arrives, the coordinator meets with them within 2 weeks to onboard them, share the agreement their predecessor signed, and review their institution's commitments. Do not wait for them to discover the platform on their own.

**NGOs sent junior staff.** The country director attended the first convening. The roadmap workshop was attended by a program officer. The first progress check meeting draws a project assistant. Each step down in seniority reduces the organization's decision-making power at the table. Address this directly: "The constitutive agreement was signed by [name/title]. We need representatives with equivalent authority to attend working sessions."

**Activities are technically completed but meaningless.** An organization reports "completed" on an activity, but the actual output does not meet the intent. Example: "Train community monitors" was completed by holding a single 2-hour presentation. The roadmap needs quality criteria for key activities, not just completion checkboxes. Add these retroactively if they were not included in the design workshops.

## Week 13: Course Correction

Week 13 is a single meeting with one purpose: honest assessment. Not celebration. Not blame. Assessment.

### Who attends

The coordination core only. 3-5 institutions: the government co-president, the indigenous authority co-president, the lead NGO, the technical secretariat coordinator, and one academic representative. This is not a plenary. This is the operational leadership making hard calls.

### The agenda (3 hours)

**Hour 1: Data review.** Walk through the progress metrics from Weeks 11-12. Identify the 3 biggest wins and the 3 biggest failures. Be specific. "The environmental sub-table completed 5 of 8 activities on time because Organization X delivered their monitoring data ahead of schedule" is useful. "Things are generally going well" is not.

**Hour 2: Structural assessment.** Ask five questions:

1. Are the sub-tables correctly configured, or do we need to merge, split, or create new ones?
2. Is the meeting frequency right? Too many meetings create fatigue. Too few let things drift.
3. Which organizations have effectively disengaged? What is the intervention strategy for each?
4. Is the accountability system producing behavior change, or are organizations ignoring notifications?
5. Do any roadmap activities need to be removed, reassigned, or redefined?

**Hour 3: Decisions and assignments.** Every structural issue identified in Hour 2 gets assigned to someone with a deadline. "Coordinator will meet with [disengaged organization] by [date] to determine whether they remain committed." "Sub-table X will be merged with Sub-table Y. Coordinator will notify affected organizations by [date]." "Roadmap activities 14, 27, and 53 are removed because no organization is willing to lead them."

### The honest conversation

This meeting is where you say the things that cannot be said in a plenary. That the private sector representatives have not attended a single sub-table session. That one NGO signed the constitutive agreement but has contributed nothing. That the government's planning department is duplicating the roadmap process with their own internal plan instead of using the platform's.

These conversations happen at the coordination core level because the relationships are strong enough to absorb candor. Raising them in a plenary of 22 organizations creates defensiveness and political theater.

### Output

A two-page course correction memo distributed to all signatories. What is working. What is being adjusted. What is expected from each organization in the next quarter. Signed by the co-presidents.

## The Five Things That Will Break

No matter how well you execute this playbook, these five problems will appear. They are structural, not situational. Plan for them.

### 1. Staff turnover kills institutional memory.

Government officials rotate every 1-2 years. NGO program staff turn over with project cycles. Every time a new person arrives, they re-litigate decisions their predecessor agreed to. "Why did we commit to this?" "I don't think this is our priority."

**Mitigation:** Keep an onboarding packet for every signatory organization. One page: what the platform is, what their organization committed to, who signed, and the current status of their activities. When the coordinator learns of a personnel change (and they must learn of it quickly, through their network, not wait for it to surface), they deliver the packet within two weeks and walk the new person through it in person.

### 2. The second meeting problem.

Organizations attend the first convening because it is novel and politically visible. They skip the second meeting because nothing visible happened between the first and the second. The gap between Meeting 1 and Meeting 2 is where platforms die.

**Mitigation:** Produce a visible deliverable between the first and second meetings. A signed constitutive agreement. A draft roadmap. A published stakeholder map. Something tangible that stakeholders receive and can show their superiors. "The platform we joined has already produced X." This playbook is sequenced to ensure that Weeks 3-8 produce continuous visible outputs.

### 3. Government capture.

The provincial government will gradually try to absorb the platform into their institutional structure. They will house the secretariat in their planning department. They will start issuing invitations from the prefect's letterhead only. They will schedule meetings when it suits their calendar, not the platform's.

**Mitigation:** Co-governance is not optional. The co-presidency model (government + indigenous authority) structurally prevents single-institution capture. The constitutive agreement should specify that invitations are issued jointly, the secretariat reports to the coordination core (not to the government), and meeting scheduling is a coordination core decision. If you notice the government starting to monopolize the platform's communications, address it immediately in the coordination core.

### 4. NGO observation trap.

NGOs, especially large INGOs, prefer to observe multi-stakeholder processes rather than commit to them. They attend meetings, take notes, report to their donors that they "participate in the governance platform," but they do not assign roadmap activities to their teams. They do not share their implementation data. They keep their project plans separate from the platform's roadmap.

**Mitigation:** The constitutive agreement must require named activity commitments from every signatory. During roadmap workshops, ask NGO representatives directly: "Which of your ongoing projects align with this sub-table's priorities? Which specific outputs will you deliver through this platform?" If they cannot name any, they are observers and should be classified as such, with all the reduced influence that status implies.

### 5. Private sector ghost membership.

A company signs the constitutive agreement for reputational purposes, designates a CSR coordinator as their representative, and that person never attends. The company's name appears on the membership list, but their seat is empty at every working session.

**Mitigation:** Two approaches. First, engage private sector actors through their operational interests, not their CSR departments. The supply chain manager cares about traceability. The environmental compliance officer cares about regulatory risk. The operations director cares about community relations that affect project timelines. Second, set a participation threshold in the constitutive agreement: organizations that miss more than 3 consecutive meetings without justification lose active membership status. This threshold creates a real consequence for non-participation.

## What Happens After Day 90

By Week 13, you should have:

- A signed constitutive agreement with 18-25 organizations.
- A roadmap with 60-100 specific, assigned, time-bound activities.
- An accountability system that tracks and makes visible every organization's progress.
- A coordination core that meets monthly and makes real operational decisions.
- A first progress check with baseline data.
- A course correction memo with specific adjustments.

The next phase is sustaining execution across the first full year. That requires institutionalization: a prefectoral decree (as in Morona Santiago), multi-funder support for the secretariat, and demonstrating to every stakeholder that the platform produces coordination outcomes they cannot achieve alone.

That is a different playbook. This one got you to the starting line.
