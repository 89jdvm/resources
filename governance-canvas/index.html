The Governance Canvas

Design your multi-stakeholder platform in one page — a framework distilled from building 3 Cooperation Tables coordinating 22+ organizations each in the Ecuadorian Amazon.

By Juan Diego Villacís — 3 Cooperation Tables designed and institutionalized (Morona Santiago, Orellana, Sucumbíos)

Download Markdown

Why This Canvas Exists

Most multi-stakeholder platforms fail within two years. Not because of lack of funding or political will — but because they never designed their governance architecture before convening. They skip the hard questions: What can this platform actually decide? What happens when stakeholders stop showing up? Who is accountable for execution?

I've built 3 Cooperation Tables across the Ecuadorian Amazon. Each one coordinates 22+ organizations — public sector, indigenous authorities, NGOs, academia, and private sector — around a shared roadmap with quarterly targets. This canvas captures the 8 structural questions that determined whether each platform succeeded or became another "talking shop."

How to use this

Fill it in before your first stakeholder meeting — not after. The blocks you can't fill are your biggest risks. Share the completed canvas with your coordination core as your design document. Revisit every 6 months as political contexts shift.

The 8 Blocks — In Depth

Block 1: Territorial Scope

Define the exact boundary. Multi-stakeholder platforms fail when the territorial scope is too vague ("the Amazon region") or too ambitious ("the entire watershed"). Be specific: province, canton, protected area, indigenous territory, or watershed boundary.

The Mesa de Cooperación in Morona Santiago covers the entire province — 25,000 km², 12 cantons, population ~200,000. It overlaps with territories of the Shuar and Achuar nations. The territorial scope was defined by the provincial government boundary, giving it administrative legitimacy, but it required indigenous governance structures (life plans, consultative assemblies) to be recognized as co-equal inputs.

If you can't define the territorial scope clearly, you're not ready to build the platform.

Block 2: Problem Statement

Not "sustainable development" — that's a goal, not a problem. What is the specific thing that is broken, duplicated, or missing because organizations are not coordinating?

In Orellana and Sucumbíos, the problem was that conservation and development organizations were operating in the same territory with no shared execution roadmap. The provincial government had infrastructure plans that conflicted with conservation zones. NGOs were implementing projects in the same communities without knowing about each other. Indigenous organizations were consulted sporadically by different actors at different times, creating consultation fatigue and contradictory commitments.

Write the problem in one sentence. If you need more than one sentence, you haven't understood the problem yet.

Block 3: Stakeholder Map

Not "who would be nice to include." Who MUST be there, or the platform's decisions carry no weight? Five categories: Public Sector, Indigenous Authorities, Civil Society/NGOs, Academia, Private Sector.

Common mistake

Including everyone who asks. A platform with 50 organizations is a conference, not a governance body. 18-25 is the operational sweet spot — enough for legitimacy, small enough for decision-making.

Block 4: Mandate & Decision Authority

This is the block most platforms skip — and the reason most platforms die within a year. If the platform has no authority to do anything, stakeholders stop coming after the second meeting.

Four levels: Information sharing → Advisory → Coordination → Governance. Each requires increasing levels of formalization and stakeholder commitment.

The Morona Santiago Cooperation Table operates at Level 3 (Coordination) with elements of Level 4 (Governance) through its constitutive agreement — signed by the provincial prefect, indigenous federation presidents, NGO country directors, and academic rectors.

If your mandate is Level 1 (information sharing only), you don't need a platform — you need a mailing list.

Block 5: Execution Instrument

Every effective platform needs one shared document — the Hoja de Ruta (Roadmap): a multi-year action plan broken into quarterly targets, organized by thematic sub-tables, with named responsible organizations per activity.

Our roadmaps: 79 activities in Orellana, 93 in Sucumbíos. The roadmap is not a wish list. If an activity has no named responsible organization and no timeline, it's not in the roadmap.

Block 6: Accountability Mechanism

Four layers — deploy all four: Visibility (public dashboard), Notification (automated reminders), Reporting (quarterly reviews), Escalation (consequences for non-delivery).

If no one is tracking, no one is accountable. And if no one is accountable, the platform is a talking shop.

Block 7: Meeting Architecture

Three-tier structure: Plenary Assembly (biannual, all stakeholders, strategic decisions), Sub-table Working Sessions (quarterly, 6-10 orgs, detailed planning), Coordination Core (monthly, 3-5 institutions, operational management).

Common mistake

Holding plenaries too frequently. When you gather 22+ organizations every quarter, the meetings become performative. Reserve plenaries for real decisions. Use sub-tables for the actual work.

Block 8: Sustainability Model

Most platforms die when the project that funded them ends. Use at least two strategies: Institutional embedding, Multi-funder architecture, Stakeholder co-financing, Technical secretariat.

The Morona Santiago Cooperation Table was institutionalized through a formal prefectoral decree, making it a permanent function of the provincial government. When the political administration changed, the decree provided legal continuity.

If your exit strategy is "hopefully the next project will fund it," you haven't built a governance platform — you've built a project component.

Implementing This in Your Territory?

This canvas gives you the framework. The political choreography — who to approach first, how to handle the official who wants control, how to get indigenous authorities to see the platform as an extension of their governance — that's where it gets complex.

I've built this across 3 provinces with 22+ organizations each. I've made the mistakes so you don't have to.
