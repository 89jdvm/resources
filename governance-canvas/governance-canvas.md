# The Governance Canvas

## Design Your Multi-Stakeholder Platform in One Page

**By Juan Diego Villacís.** Practitioner-Scholar for AI + Amazonian Governance

I designed and institutionalized 3 Cooperation Tables for Conservation and Sustainable Development across the Ecuadorian Amazon. Each one coordinates 22+ organizations: public sector, indigenous authorities, NGOs, academia, and private sector. Around a shared roadmap (hoja de ruta) with quarterly targets.

This canvas captures what I learned building those three platforms. It is not a theoretical framework. It is a distillation of what actually works when you put 22 organizations in a room and need them to coordinate. Not just meet.

---

## What This Canvas Is For

Before you design a multi-stakeholder platform, you need to answer 8 questions. Most platforms fail because they skip at least 3 of them. Usually the hard ones about mandate, accountability, and what happens when stakeholders stop showing up.

This canvas forces you to answer all 8 before your first meeting. Print it. Fill it in. If you can't fill a box, that's your biggest risk.

---

## The 8 Blocks

### Block 1: TERRITORIAL SCOPE

**What geography and population does this platform govern?**

Define the exact boundary. Multi-stakeholder platforms fail when the territorial scope is too vague ("the Amazon region") or too ambitious ("the entire watershed"). Be specific: province, canton, protected area, indigenous territory, or watershed boundary.

Questions to answer:
- What is the formal administrative boundary?
- Does it overlap with indigenous territorial claims? Which nations?
- What other governance structures already operate here? (municipal government, protected area management, indigenous self-governance)
- What is the population? How many communities?

Example from practice: The Mesa de Cooperación in Morona Santiago covers the entire province. 25,000 km², 12 cantons, population ~200,000. It overlaps with territories of the Shuar and Achuar nations. The territorial scope was defined by the provincial government boundary, which gave it administrative legitimacy, but it required indigenous governance structures (life plans, consultative assemblies) to be recognized as co-equal inputs.

**If you can't define the territorial scope clearly, you're not ready to build the platform.**

---

### Block 2: THE PROBLEM STATEMENT

**What specific coordination failure does this platform exist to solve?**

Not "sustainable development" . That is a goal, not a problem. What is the specific thing that is broken, duplicated, or missing because organizations are not coordinating?

Questions to answer:
- What decisions are being made in isolation that should be coordinated?
- Where is effort being duplicated across organizations?
- What information exists in one organization that others need but don't have?
- What governance vacuum is allowing conflicts, overlaps, or missed opportunities?

Example from practice: In Orellana and Sucumbíos, the problem was that conservation and development organizations were operating in the same territory with no shared execution roadmap. The provincial government had infrastructure plans that conflicted with conservation zones. NGOs were implementing projects in the same communities without knowing about each other. Indigenous organizations were consulted sporadically by different actors at different times, creating consultation fatigue and contradictory commitments.

**Write the problem in one sentence. If you need more than one sentence, you haven't understood the problem yet.**

---

### Block 3: STAKEHOLDER MAP

**Who must be at the table for this platform to have legitimacy?**

Not "who would be nice to include." Who MUST be there, or the platform's decisions carry no weight?

Five stakeholder categories (from the Cooperation Table model):

| Category | Role | Legitimacy Source |
|----------|------|-------------------|
| **Public Sector** | Provincial/municipal government, line ministries (environment, agriculture, planning) | Administrative authority, budget allocation, policy enforcement |
| **Indigenous Authorities** | Territorial governance bodies, nationalities, federations | Territorial sovereignty, FPIC rights, historical legitimacy, life plans |
| **Civil Society / NGOs** | International and local conservation, development, and rights organizations | Technical capacity, donor relationships, implementation experience |
| **Academia** | Universities, research centers | Technical knowledge, data, monitoring capacity |
| **Private Sector** | Companies, cooperatives, producer associations operating in the territory | Economic activity, supply chain influence, employment |

Questions to answer:
- For each category: which specific organizations? (names, not generic labels)
- Who has formal authority vs. practical influence?
- Who has veto power? (indigenous FPIC, government permits, donor conditions)
- Who is missing but whose absence will undermine legitimacy?
- What is the expected number of organizations? (Our tables averaged 22-23)

**Common mistake:** Including everyone who asks. A platform with 50 organizations is a conference, not a governance body. 18-25 is the operational sweet spot. Enough for legitimacy, small enough for decision-making.

---

### Block 4: MANDATE AND DECISION AUTHORITY

**What can this platform actually decide, recommend, or enforce?**

This is the block most platforms skip. And the reason most platforms die within a year. If the platform has no authority to do anything, stakeholders will stop coming after the second meeting.

Four levels of mandate (ascending):

| Level | Authority | Example |
|-------|-----------|---------|
| **Information sharing** | Platform collects and distributes information. No binding decisions. | "We share project updates quarterly." |
| **Advisory** | Platform makes recommendations to decision-makers. Not binding. | "The Mesa recommends the provincial government align its infrastructure plan with the conservation roadmap." |
| **Coordination** | Platform assigns responsibilities and tracks execution. Morally binding. | "Organization X commits to complete Activity Y by Q3. Progress is tracked and reported." |
| **Governance** | Platform makes binding decisions that stakeholders must follow. Legally or institutionally binding. | "The constitutive agreement establishes that no new extractive concession can be granted in Zone A without platform consensus." |

Questions to answer:
- What level of mandate does each stakeholder type actually accept?
- What enforcement mechanism exists? (institutional, social, financial)
- Is the mandate formalized in a constitutive agreement or MoU?
- Who signs? At what level? (Director? Minister? President of federation?)

Example from practice: The Morona Santiago Cooperation Table operates at Level 3 (Coordination) with elements of Level 4 (Governance) through its constitutive agreement. The constitutive agreement. Signed by the provincial prefect, indigenous federation presidents, NGO country directors, and academic rectors. It establishes the platform's authority to assign and track roadmap activities. It does not have legal enforcement power, but the social accountability of 22+ signatories creates strong compliance pressure.

**If your mandate is Level 1 (information sharing only), you don't need a platform. You need a mailing list.**

---

### Block 5: EXECUTION INSTRUMENT

**What shared document drives the platform's work?**

Every effective multi-stakeholder platform needs a single, shared execution instrument. The one document that all stakeholders reference and that tracks what needs to happen, who is responsible, and when.

In the Cooperation Table model, this is the **Hoja de Ruta** (Roadmap): a multi-year action plan broken into quarterly targets, organized by thematic sub-tables (e.g., Environmental, Socio-Economic, Governance), with named responsible organizations per activity.

Anatomy of the Hoja de Ruta:

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Time horizon** | 2-3 years (ours: 2026-2028) |
| **Thematic sub-tables** | 3-5 working groups by topic (e.g., Environment, Agroproductive, Social, Governance) |
| **Activities** | Specific, measurable actions (not "promote sustainability" . Instead: "establish 3 community monitoring stations by Q2 2027") |
| **Responsible organization(s)** | Named org per activity. Not "the platform" |
| **Timeline** | Quarterly milestones |
| **Status tracking** | Pending / In Progress / Completed / Delayed |
| **Budget/resource needs** | What resources each activity requires and where they come from |

Questions to answer:
- Does a shared execution document already exist? (Sometimes it does. Buried in a Google Drive.)
- How many activities? (Our roadmaps: 79 in Orellana, 93 in Sucumbíos)
- Who updates it? How often?
- How do stakeholders see their own progress vs. others'?

**The roadmap is not a wish list. If an activity has no named responsible organization and no timeline, it's not in the roadmap.**

### Block 6: ACCOUNTABILITY MECHANISM

**How do you ensure stakeholders actually do what they committed to?**

This is the operational core of the platform. Without accountability, the roadmap collects dust. Which is exactly what happens to most multi-stakeholder agreements.

Four accountability layers (deploy all four):

| Layer | Mechanism | Frequency |
|-------|-----------|-----------|
| **Visibility** | Public dashboard showing activity status by organization | Always-on |
| **Notification** | Automated reminders when deadlines approach or pass | Weekly/Monthly |
| **Reporting** | Quarterly progress reviews in plenary session. Each org reports on their activities | Quarterly |
| **Escalation** | What happens when an organization consistently fails to deliver? Social accountability, formal communication, conditional participation | As needed |

Questions to answer:
- Who tracks progress? (A coordinator? A shared system?)
- How is progress made visible to all stakeholders? (Dashboard? Report? WhatsApp group?)
- What is the escalation path for non-compliance?
- Is there positive recognition for organizations that deliver?

Example from practice: We built a notification engine that sends automated email reminders to responsible organizations when activities approach their deadline, and escalation notices when activities are overdue. A public dashboard shows the status of all activities by province, sub-table, and quarter. Making it impossible for an organization to hide non-delivery.

**If no one is tracking, no one is accountable. And if no one is accountable, the platform is a talking shop.**

---

### Block 7: MEETING ARCHITECTURE

**How often, in what format, and with what decision authority do stakeholders meet?**

Multi-stakeholder platforms tend to either meet too often (participant fatigue) or too rarely (no momentum). The meeting architecture must balance representation with efficiency.

Three-tier meeting structure (from the Cooperation Table model):

| Tier | Who Attends | Frequency | Purpose |
|------|-------------|-----------|---------|
| **Plenary Assembly** | All stakeholders (22+ orgs) | Biannual (every 6 months) | Approve/revise roadmap. Review progress. Make strategic decisions. Sign agreements. |
| **Sub-table Working Sessions** | Stakeholders relevant to each thematic area (6-10 orgs) | Quarterly | Detailed activity planning. Technical coordination. Problem-solving. |
| **Coordination Core** | 3-5 key institutions (government, indigenous authority, lead NGO) + technical secretariat | Monthly | Operational coordination. Resolve blockers. Prepare sub-table agendas. |

Questions to answer:
- Who convenes each tier? (Legitimacy of the convener matters enormously.)
- Where do meetings happen? (Rotating between stakeholder territories builds ownership.)
- What is the decision-making protocol? (Consensus? Majority? Veto rights for indigenous authorities?)
- How are meeting outcomes documented and distributed?
- What happens when a key stakeholder doesn't attend?

**Common mistake:** Holding plenaries too frequently. When you gather 22+ organizations every quarter, the meetings become performative. People report but don't decide. Reserve plenaries for real decisions. Use sub-tables for the actual work.**

---

### Block 8: SUSTAINABILITY MODEL

**How does this platform survive beyond the current funding cycle?**

Most multi-stakeholder platforms die when the project that funded them ends. If the platform depends on a single donor, NGO, or government administration, it has a single point of failure.

Four sustainability strategies (use at least two):

| Strategy | How It Works | Risk |
|----------|-------------|------|
| **Institutional embedding** | Platform becomes part of formal government structure (ordinance, decree) | Survives political transitions. Risk: new administration deprioritizes. |
| **Multi-funder architecture** | Platform supported by multiple donors/orgs. No single dependency | Resilient. Risk: coordination overhead. |
| **Stakeholder co-financing** | Each stakeholder contributes resources (staff time, logistics, data) instead of cash | Builds ownership. Risk: free-rider problem. |
| **Technical secretariat** | A permanent coordination function hosted by a neutral institution (university, regional body) | Continuity of operations. Risk: neutrality perception. |

Questions to answer:
- Who currently funds the platform operations?
- What happens if that funder exits?
- Is there a legal instrument (ordinance, MoU) that gives the platform institutional life beyond the project?
- Which stakeholders would continue participating without external funding?
- Can the technical secretariat function be absorbed by a permanent institution?

Example from practice: The Morona Santiago Cooperation Table was institutionalized through a formal prefectoral decree, making it a permanent function of the provincial government. When the political administration changed, the decree provided legal continuity. In Orellana and Sucumbíos, the constitutive agreement, signed by 22+ organizations, creates social and institutional pressure for continuity beyond the Conservation International contract.

**If your exit strategy is "hopefully the next project will fund it," you haven't built a governance platform. You have built a project component.**

## How to Use This Canvas

1. **Print or copy the 8-block template below**
2. **Fill it in before your first stakeholder meeting**. Not after
3. **The blocks you can't fill are your biggest risks**. Address them before launching
4. **Share the filled canvas with your coordination core**. It becomes your design document
5. **Revisit every 6 months**. Governance platforms evolve as political contexts shift
